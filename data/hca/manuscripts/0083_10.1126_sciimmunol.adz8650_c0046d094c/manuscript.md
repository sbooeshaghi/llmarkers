Editor’s summary
The gut immune system is highly complex because of constant exposure to food and microbial products and the distinct functions of various intestinal segments. Using single-cell transcriptomics, Fenton et al . profiled mononuclear phagocytes in the human ileal and colonic laminae propriae from both healthy and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)–associated tissue. In addition to identifying putative dendritic cell (DC) precursors, differences in macrophage and DC subset proportions were observed between the ileum and colon, with type 2 conventional DCs in particular acquiring site-specific features. In patients with IBD, less differentiated macrophages and a population of CCR7 + cDCs were enriched in inflamed laminae propriae. This analysis provides a resource of human mononuclear phagocyte heterogeneity in healthy and IBD-inflamed intestinal laminae propriae. —Claire Olingy
Abstract
Understanding of mononuclear phagocyte (MNP) diversity in the human intestine and the alterations this compartment undergoes in inflammation remains incomplete. Here, we used single-cell RNA sequencing, cellular indexing of trancriptomes and epitopes by sequencing, flow cytometry, and imaging to explore MNP heterogeneity in human ileal and colonic laminae propriae (LPs) in health and Crohn’s disease (CD). In addition to monocytes, macrophage subsets, and conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s) and cDC2s, we found a CD1c + cDC subset with transcriptional features of DC3s. Using computational tools, we identified monocyte-to-macrophage trajectories as well as putative subset–specific DC precursors. We further showed that LP CCR7 + cDCs are increased in CD and provided evidence that these cells arise from intestinal cDC2s/DC3s but not cDC1s. Collectively, these findings extend our current understanding of intestinal MNP diversity and development, highlighting both tissue-specific and inflammation-induced changes in MNP composition and function.

INTRODUCTION
The mononuclear phagocyte (MNP) family consists of conventional dendritic cells (cDCs), monocytes, and macrophages, each of which plays specific roles in immune responses, tissue homeostasis, and inflammation ( 1 – 3 ). Whereas cDCs are the principal cells involved in the induction and shaping of adaptive immune responses ( 4 ), tissue-resident macrophages are primarily involved in maintaining local tissue homeostasis, defense against infection, and tissue repair ( 5 , 6 ). Recent studies have highlighted considerable heterogeneity among MNPs and demonstrated that these cells develop distinct functions depending on the niches in which they reside ( 7 – 11 ).
The intestine is continually exposed to food and microbial products that are essential for maintaining health ( 12 , 13 ). The intestinal immune system must respond appropriately to these products to maintain tissue homeostasis, while retaining the ability to mount effective immunity to intestinal pathogens. Consequently, the intestine contains the largest and most diverse immune compartment in the body. MNPs are found in all anatomical layers of the human intestine, including the lamina propria (LP), the muscularis mucosa, and the organized gut-associated lymphoid tissues (GALTs) ( 14 ). Studies in mice have highlighted the different roles that MNP subsets play in intestinal homeostasis and have shown that MNP composition and function are influenced by the anatomical location in which they reside ( 1 , 2 , 11 , 15 , 16 ). Consistent with this, single-cell transcriptomic analyses suggest considerable heterogeneity between the macrophage compartment of the human colonic mucosa and muscularis mucosa ( 17 – 19 ). Whereas these findings highlight the importance of assessing MNP diversity in different intestinal niches, this has not been possible to date because of challenges in isolating human LP tissue free from contaminating submucosa (SM) and GALT.
Furthermore, the human gastrointestinal tract is not homogenous but consists of several anatomically and functionally distinct segments. For example, the small intestine, whose surface is characterized by finger-like projections termed villi, is the major site of food digestion and absorption. Conversely, the colonic surface consists of a flattened mucosa and harbors the largest number and variety of microbes ( 20 , 21 ). As a result, the concentrations of dietary and microbial products and metabolites, many of which have direct impacts on local immune cell development and function, vary greatly along the length of the intestine. How variations in intestinal anatomy, function, luminal contents and inflammation affect local MNP diversity in humans remains incompletely understood.
Here, we used our recently developed technique to isolate intestinal LP free from contaminating GALT and SM ( 22 , 23 ) to assess the phenotypic, transcriptional, and developmental diversity of MNPs in human ileal and colonic LPs. Our results provide insights into intestinal LP MNP diversity and reveal site-specific alterations in this compartment in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).
RESULTS
Single-cell transcriptional profiling of MNPs in the human intestinal LP
To assess MNP diversity in the intestinal LP, healthy mucosae were obtained from surgical resections from patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) taken >10 cm from the tumor margin. They were then processed to remove contaminating GALT and SM, as described previously ( 22 , 23 ). After LP digestion, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on sorted CD45 + CD3 − CD19 − HLADR int/+ cells from six colonic LP samples and four paired ileal LP samples ( Fig. 1A and table S1). Distinct clusters of CD3E + T cells, CD79A + B cells, VWF + endothelial cells, MS4A2 + mast cells, COL3A1 + stromal cells, and NRXN1 + glia were excluded from further analysis (fig. S1A). Major histocompatibility complex II (MHCII) genes were expressed by one “supercluster” and two peripheral clusters (fig. S1B), which were computationally isolated and reclustered to dissect their transcriptional identity. These 28,758 MHCII + cells comprised distinct clusters of IL3RA + plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), CLEC9A + cDC1s, and FCGR3A + nonclassical monocytes, as well as a central supercluster ( Fig. 1B ). Cells in distinct regions of this supercluster expressed the monocyte-associated marker S100A8 , the macrophage-associated marker C1QA , CSFR1 or the cDC2-associated marker CD1C , and FLT3 ( Fig. 1C ). The myeloid marker CD14 was expressed by S100A8 + and C1QA + cells but was also present on some CD1C + cells ( Fig. 1C ). Consistent with this, flow cytometric analysis of colon LP CD45 + human leukocyte antigen (HLA)–DR + cells showed the presence of cells expressing CD1c or CD14 alone, as well as cells expressing variable levels of both markers ( Fig. 1D ).

To determine the identity of cells in this supercluster, we reclustered them at high resolution into 39 subclusters. All clusters were present in all samples, albeit in slightly different proportions (fig. S1C). Manual annotation based on known monocyte-, macrophage-, and cDC2-associated genes ( 24 – 28 ) was used to assign each of the 39 clusters as either monocytes/macrophages (monos/macs) or cDC2/DC3-like ( Fig. 1E ). Cluster numbers were assigned on the basis of manual annotation of transcriptionally related clusters, comprising monocytes (clusters 1 to 3), mono/mac intermediates (clusters 4 to 14), and differentiated macrophages (clusters 15 to 20) ( Fig. 1E ). Clusters 1 to 3 expressed the classical monocyte transcription factor (TF) ZBTB16 ( 29 ), clusters 4 to 14 expressed intermediate levels of signature genes associated with both monocytes and macrophages, and clusters 15 to 20 expressed high levels of genes associated with mature macrophages, such as SEPP1 , MERTK , and MAF , together with TFs involved in tissue-resident macrophage development such as ID3 ( Fig. 1E ) ( 30 ). A second group of clusters (clusters 21 to 39) could be identified on the basis of their expression of CD1C . Of these, clusters 21 to 35 expressed low levels of monocyte- and macrophage-associated genes and high levels of the cDC2-associated genes AP1S3 , FLT3 , SEPT6 , and IRF4 ( Fig. 1E ) ( 31 , 32 ), whereas clusters 36 to 39 expressed both cDC2-associated genes, including FLT3 , IRF4 , and CD1C , and mono/mac-associated genes, including CD14 , CD163 , MERTK , S100A9 , and C5AR1 (CD88), albeit at lower levels than observed in the mono/mac clusters ( Fig. 1E ). Given that DC3s have been reported to coexpress cDC2 and monocyte markers ( 33 , 34 ), we designated clusters 21 to 39 as cDC2/DC3-like.
Pseudobulk principal components analysis (PCA) on each subcluster using transcriptional input from a pan-tissue dataset of human cDC2s, DC3s, monocytes, and macrophages ( 35 ) broadly confirmed our designation of the 39 subclusters as either monos/macs or cDC2/DC3-like ( Fig. 1F ). Consistent with this, the mono/mac and cDC2/DC3-like subclusters mapped to distinct sides of the supercluster uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), except for the proliferating mono/mac subcluster 11, which localized near the proliferating cDC2/DC3-like subclusters 21 and 39 ( Fig. 1, E and G ).
The intestinal LP contains diverse monocyte-derived mature macrophages
To further assess intestinal mono/mac diversity, we separately reclustered and analyzed the ileal and colonic LP mono/mac clusters using trajectory space (tSPACE), an algorithm that maps cells along nearest-neighbor pathways to all other cells in a population, allowing unsupervised visualization of developmental sequences ( 36 ). Two-dimensional (2D) representation of a 3D tSPACE UMAP (Flat tUMAP) identified 11 clusters (M1 to M11) ( Fig. 2A and movie S1) present at varying proportions in paired ileal and colonic LP samples (fig. S2A). Cluster M11 consisted of only 16 cells, expressed high levels of cell cycle–associated genes including MKI67 and KIAA0101 (fig. S2B), and were not analyzed further. Cluster M1, located at one end of the tSPACE ( Fig. 2A and movie S1), expressed the highest levels of the monocyte-associated genes S100A8 , S100A9 , FCN1 , and VCAN ( 25 , 37 ), expressed the lowest level of HLA genes, and did not express the mature macrophage genes CD209 and C1QC ( Fig. 2B and fig. S2B), indicating that M1 cells represent recently recruited monocytes. tSPACE and pseudotime analysis using cluster M1 as a trajectory starting point demonstrated that clusters M2 and M3, which expressed intermediate levels of monocyte-associated genes ( Fig. 2B ), lay directly downstream of cluster M1 in the trajectory, whereas clusters M4 and M5, which lacked expression of these genes ( Fig. 2B ), lay downstream of clusters M2 and M3 ( Fig. 2C and movie S1), suggesting that M2 and M3 represented early clusters and M4 and M5 late-intermediate clusters. Clusters M6 to M8 expressed mature macrophage markers and lay on distinct branches at the opposite end of the trajectory ( Fig. 2, A to C ; fig. S2B; and movie S1), whereas clusters M9 and M10, which branched off cluster M8, had some features of mature macrophages but expressed low levels of CD209 and CD163 ( Fig. 2B and fig. S2B). Trajectory analysis also indicated that clusters M6 and M7 to M10 may derive from distinct intermediates ( Fig. 2, A and C , and movie S1). Although such analysis is inherently predictive, these results suggest the presence of distinct populations of monocyte-derived macrophages in the human intestinal LP that locally differentiate.

Mature macrophage subsets are transcriptionally conserved across the ileum and colon
Given the limited number of cells in clusters M9 and M10, we focused our analysis of differentiated macrophages on clusters M6 to M8. Cluster M6 differentially expressed 1482 genes compared with cluster M7 or M8 (fig. S2C and data file S1), including genes encoding the C-type lectins CLEC7A and CLEC12A, as well as TNFSF10 that encodes tumor necrosis factor (TNF)–related apoptosis-inducing ligand ( Fig. 2, D and E , and data file S1). Gene ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated that cluster M6 was enriched in genes involved in positive regulation of interleukin-1 production, as well as in translation and ribosome biogenesis indicating high translational activity ( Fig. 2F ).
Cluster M7 differentially expressed 1034 genes compared with either cluster M6 or M8 (fig. S2C and data file S1), including several chemokines and the tissue repair and angiogenesis-associated genes VEGFA , PDGFB , and PTGS2 encoding cyclooxygenase-2 ( Fig. 2, D and E , and data file S1). Cluster M7 also expressed FOLR2 , MAF , and LYVE1 ( Fig. 2D ), which in mice are associated with perivascular macrophages ( 38 , 39 ). Consistent with this, GO analysis demonstrated that M7 macrophages were enriched in pathways associated with vascular development and regulation of angiogenesis, in addition to inflammatory response and responsiveness to cytokines and molecules of bacterial origin ( Fig. 2F ). FOLR2 + macrophages are found in the middle and crypt region of the human colonic LP and in the SM ( 19 ); however, cluster M7 lacked expression of the submucosal macrophage markers COLEC12 ( 18 ) and MARCO ( 19 ) ( Fig. 2D ), consistent with efficient removal of SM in our LP isolation protocol.
Cluster M8 expressed 1140 genes at significantly higher levels than cluster M6 or M7, including the metalloprotease MMP12 ; LGALS3 that encodes galectin-3; and the lipid metabolism–associated genes PLA2G7 , LIPA , and APOC1 ( Fig. 2, D and E ; fig. S2C; and data file S1). Consistent with this, M8 macrophages were enriched in pathways associated with lipid transport ( Fig. 2F ). Cluster M8 also expressed high levels of ACP5 , a marker of differentiated macrophages locate at the tip of human colonic crypts ( 18 , 19 ).
There were very few differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the ileum and colon for any of the clusters ( Fig. 2G and data file S2). Although several genes encoding immune modulatory molecules were differentially expressed by all three clusters in either the ileum or colon ( Fig. 2, H and I ), the relevance of these in regulating local immune homeostasis remains to be explored. Thus, M6 to M8 appear to represent three distinct macrophage subsets in the LP, whose transcriptional profile is largely conserved across the ileum and colon.
To determine how clusters M6 to M8 related to recently described colonic macrophage populations ( 18 ), we embedded the signature score (top 50 DEGs) for each cluster onto a UMAP of a previously published colonic dataset (fig. S2D) ( 18 ). Consistent with our findings, the signature score for each cluster primarily overlapped with clusters suggested previously to represent tissue-resident macrophage subsets (fig. S2D) ( 18 ). Whereas the signature score of M7 was primarily located at the top left of the UMAP, corresponding with cluster 12 and half of cluster 10 in the Domanska et al. dataset ( 18 ), the signature scores of clusters M6 and M8 largely overlapped with one another. We were unable to determine why M6 and M8 could not be distinguished in that dataset.
Mono/mac intermediates increase in inflamed LPs of patients with IBD
To identify surface antigens that may help depict the stages of intestinal monocyte differentiation by flow cytometry, we used a LEGENDScreen assay to screen for surface marker expression on colonic CD14 + CD1c lo MNPs (fig. S3, A and B). CD11c, CD11a, CD206, and CD55 showed heterogenous expression levels on CD14 + CD1c lo cells (fig. S3B), and we thus used antibodies recognizing these surface markers, together with CD14 and CD1c, to perform cellular indexing of trancriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) analysis of MNPs from three colonic LP samples ( Fig. 3A ) and one ileal LP sample (fig. S3C). On the basis of surface marker and transcriptional analysis, we generated an antibody panel designed to distinguish monocyte-derived early intermediates, late intermediates, and differentiated macrophages by flow cytometry ( Fig. 3B ).

Differentiated macrophages could be divided into CD14 hi CD206 hi cells, likely to be enriched in cluster M6 and M7 macrophages; CD14 lo CD206 int cells, likely enriched in cluster M8 macrophages; and a minor population of CD14 lo CD206 lo cells, likely enriched in cluster M9 and M10 macrophages ( Fig. 3, A and C ). Flow cytometry analysis from 10 matched ileal and colonic resection samples suggested that the proportion of intermediate cells was higher in the colonic LP compared with the ileal LP ( Fig. 3, D and E ). The colonic LP also contained a higher proportion of CD14 hi CD206 hi but lower proportion of CD14 lo CD206 lo differentiated macrophages.
To assess whether the proportions of these populations change during inflammation, similar analysis was performed from colonic biopsies from healthy individuals and patients with Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) ( Fig. 3F and table S2). The presence of inflammation correlated with increased proportions of early and late intermediates, together with concomitant decreases in the proportions of both mature CD14 hi CD206 hi and CD14 lo CD206 lo macrophage populations ( Fig. 3F ). Thus, multiple stages of mono/mac differentiation can be identified in the human intestine by flow cytometry, and this differs between the ileum and colon as well as in the setting of IBD.
The human intestinal LP contains cDC1s, cDC2s, and DC3s
To explore intestinal LP cDC diversity, we computationally isolated and reclustered cDC1s and cDC2/DC3-like cells we identified ( Fig. 1 ). Cells were clustered at high resolution and visualized by tSPACE-based clustering by Flat tUMAP. All subclusters were present in both ileal and colonic LP datasets (fig. S4A). Despite regressing out cell cycle effects, several clusters located together at the top of the tUMAP were enriched in cells expressing high levels of mitotic G 2 M-S genes (fig. S4B) and relatively low levels of MHCII genes (fig. S4C), both of which are characteristics of cDC precursors. To assess the identity of the remaining clusters, we first analyzed expression of the canonical cDC1 signature genes CLEC9A , CADM1 , XCR1 , BATF3 , and IRF8 , identifying seven clusters with a distinct cDC1 signature score (fig. S4D). We then ranked the remaining clusters on the basis of their average module expression of the cDC2- or DC3-associated signature genes published by Bourdely et al. ( 40 ) (fig. S4E). This allowed us to tentatively identify cDC2s and DC3s, as well as clusters that could not be identified definitively on the basis of their cDC2/DC3 signature score, which we termed ambiguous clusters (fig. S4E). We also identified a minor population of LAMP3 + cDCs, which expressed high levels of the maturation markers CCR7 and CD40 (fig. S4F), likely representing mature cDCs with the capacity to migrate toward lymph nodes ( 41 , 42 ).
The cDC1, cDC2, and DC3 clusters were largely separated from each other in the tUMAP projection, with the ambiguous clusters positioned between cDC2s and DC3s ( Fig. 4A ). Each cDC subset expressed many DEGs, including genes supporting their designation as cDC1s, cDC2s, and DC3s (fig. S4, G and H, and data file S3). Specifically, the top DEGs for the cDC1 cluster included CLEC9A , CADM1 , and ID2 ; the cDC2 cluster expressed high levels of IRF4 , PLAC8 , and CCL22 ; and the DC3 cluster expressed high levels of C1QA , S100A9 , and CD163 ; however, the ambiguous cluster expressed genes associated with both cDC2s and DC3s (fig. S4H). GO analysis also identified terms that were distinct for each DC subset ( Fig. 4B ).

In addition to classical cDC1-associated TFs such as IRF8 , ID2 , and BATF3 , ileal and colonic cDC1s differentially expressed several other TFs including ZEB1 ( Fig. 4C ), implicated in cDC1-mediated T helper 1 (T H 1) responses ( 43 ), as well as JUN , MYC , and MYCL , implicated in cDC1 development and function ( 44 , 45 ). In addition to the cDC2-associated TF IRF4 , cDC2s selectively expressed the Notch pathway gene HES4 and NR4A3 , implicated in cDC activation ( 46 ). As expected, DC3s expressed the highest levels of macrophage-associated TFs, including MAF , MAFB , MAFF , and ZBTB16 , and also the inhibitory TFs NFKBIA and NFKBIZ ( Fig. 4C ). Ambiguous cells expressed the highest levels of KLF4 ( Fig. 4C ), a TF implicated in cDC2-mediated T H 2 responses ( 47 ). Cytokines and chemokines were also expressed in a cDC subset–specific manner including high levels of the TNF family members TNF and TNFSF11 (RANK-L) among cDC1s, high levels of CCL19 , CCL22 , and EBI3 among cDC2s, and a wide range of cytokines and chemokines by DC3s ( Fig. 4D ).
We next used the Discriminant Regulon Expression Analysis (DoRothEA) package, which infers TF activity from expression of downstream target genes ( Fig. 4E ) ( 48 ), and the Pathway RespOnsive GENes (PROGENy) package, which infers pathway activity in cells on the basis of expression levels of pathway response genes ( Fig. 4F ) ( 49 ). DoRothEA analysis suggested selective SOX2, FLI1, LEF1, and FOXA1 activity in cDC1s ( Fig. 4E ), whereas DC3s showed enhanced activity of a broad range of TFs ( Fig. 4F ). PROGENy analysis suggested that the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase pathway was particularly active in cDC1s, whereas DC3s displayed an enhanced activation of numerous pathways relative to the other cDC subsets ( Fig. 4F ).
Because DC3s coexpressed several genes associated with monos/macs, we asked how DC3s compare with the three major differentiated LP macrophage clusters M6 to M8. In addition to differing from each macrophage cluster individually, DC3s expressed a shared set of 995 genes at higher levels than all M6 to M8 clusters ( Fig. 4G and data file S4), including several chemokines/cytokines, pattern recognition receptors, and genes involved in antigen presentation pathways (fig. S4I). GO analysis on this common gene set showed enrichment for pathways of translation and aerobic respiration, indicating high metabolic activity, together with genes associated with nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling, cytokine production, and T cell activation ( Fig. 4H ). Collectively, these results highlight the distinct transcriptional activities of human intestinal DC subsets and suggest that DC3s have a more activated, proinflammatory phenotype compared with other LP cDCs and macrophages.
cDC2 and DC3 proportions differ between the colonic and ileal LPs
We next assessed whether the DC subsets showed transcriptional differences between ileal and colonic LPs. DEG analysis showed that the transcriptional profile of cDC1s differed little between the ileal and colonic LPs ( Fig. 5A and data file S5). In contrast, cDC2s differed in their expression of 421 genes, whereas DC3s differed in their expression of 167 genes between the ileum and colon and a proportion of these genes was shared between subsets ( Fig. 5, A and B , and data file S5). Given the limited number of DEGs in DC3s, we focused the GO analysis on cDC2s and found ileal cDC2s to be enriched in pathways associated with cholesterol transport and cholesterol biosynthetic processes, whereas colonic cDC2s were enriched in cellular responses to cytokines, cytokine production, and negative regulation of apoptotic process ( Fig. 5C ). Thus, cDC2s seem particularly sensitive to fine-tuning by local environmental signals.

The human intestinal LP contains precursor-like cDC1s, cDC2s, and DC3s
Previous studies have identified putative uncommitted pre-cDCs and committed precursors of cDC1s, cDC2s, and DC3s in human bone marrow, blood, and tonsils ( 25 , 29 , 40 , 50 – 52 ). To explore whether DC precursors might also be present in human intestine, we focused on the HLA-DR low DCs (fig. S4C), which, using high-resolution tSpace-based clustering, consisted of eight clusters ( Fig. 6A ). These cells were highly proliferative compared with the mature DCs ( Fig. 6B ) and expressed low levels of ITGAX (encoding CD11c) ( Fig. 6C ), features consistent with DC precursors in mice ( 53 , 54 ) and humans ( 50 , 51 ). Given that these proliferating clusters formed three distinct branches that aligned with cDC1s, cDC2s, and DC3s, we hypothesized that each branch potentially contained cDC subset–specific precursor-like cells.

To assess this possibility, we generated signatures composed of the top 50 DEGs distinguishing mature DC subsets from each other and examined how these were expressed by the various HLA low clusters. Clusters 4 and 5 shared a gene expression profile with cDC1s, cluster 7 expressed DEGs associated with cDC2s, and clusters 3 and 8 had a similar gene expression pattern to DC3s ( Fig. 6D ). RNA velocity analysis of mRNA splicing patterns ( 55 ) suggested that cluster 4 gave rise to cluster 5 and thereafter into the mature cDC1 clusters ( Fig. 6E ). Similarly, cluster 7 showed a trajectory into the mature cDC2s, whereas cluster 3 showed a trajectory toward cluster 8 and then into mature DC3s ( Fig. 6E ). Similar patterns were observed between the ileal and colonic LPs (fig. S6A). Collectively, these gene expression and splicing patterns suggest that clusters 4 and 5 may represent upstream precursors of mature cDC1s, that cluster 7 may represent an immediate precursor to cDC2s, and that clusters 3 and 8 may represent upstream precursors to DC3s.
Three adjacent clusters (clusters 1, 2, and 6) did not express DEGs specific to the mature DC subsets ( Fig. 6D ), and we hypothesized that they may be earlier, less-committed precursors. To determine whether clusters 1, 2, and 6 showed evidence of commitment to any of the DC lineages, we used the top 50 DEGs from a direct comparison of the putatively more committed clusters 5, 7, and 8 as input for a PCA of all of the HLA low clusters. Of the 150 DEGs between clusters 5, 7, and 8, 79 were also DEGs between the relevant mature cDC populations. The three putative immediate precursor clusters 5, 7, and 8 located in three distinct areas in PC1-2 ( Fig. 6F ). When overlying cells from the putative earlier clusters 2, 3, and 6 onto this PCA, they mapped to regions associated with either clusters 5, 7, or 8 ( Fig. 6G and fig. S6B), and this information was then used to align clusters along putative cDC1, cDC2, and DC3 developmental trajectories ( Fig. 6H ). In contrast, cluster 1 did not overlap with any of the putative immediate precursors of cDC1s, cDC2s, and DC3s ( Fig. 6G ).
To investigate the identity of the cells in cluster 1, we compared their gene expression profile with that of a published human bone marrow hematopoietic single-cell dataset ( 56 ). Cluster 1 showed the greatest correlation with hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), multipotent progenitors (MPPs), lympho-myeloid precursors, and early promyelocytes, together with some overlap with mature bone marrow cDCs. However, they showed no overlap with late promyelocytes, myelocytes, and classical monocytes (fig. S6C). Thus, cluster 1 may represent earlier lympho-myeloid progenitors with a potential bias toward the DC lineage.
To further assess the relationship between mature DCs and the identified precursor-like cells, we examined the expression of DC precursor and cDC subset–associated genes across the three trajectories ( Fig. 6, H and I ). Compared with other clusters, cluster 1 expressed the highest levels of KIT , similar levels of ITGAX , and the lowest levels of MHCII genes ( Fig. 6I ), consistent with the possibility that cells in this cluster may represent early progenitors ( 25 , 29 , 50 , 52 ). In agreement with the proposed trajectories, clusters associated with the putative cDC1 trajectory progressively increased their expression of cDC1-related genes BATF3 , IRF8 , CLEC9A , and CADM1 as they transitioned through clusters 2A, 3A, 4, and 5 to mature cDC1s. XCR1 expression increased during the final transition from cluster 5 to mature cDC1s ( Fig. 6I ), consistent with recent studies in mice suggesting that this marker is expressed relatively late in cDC1 differentiation ( 57 ). As expected, expression of these cDC1 genes remained low throughout the putative cDC2 and DC3 trajectories. Conversely, expression of the cDC2-associated genes IRF4 and LTB remained high across the putative cDC2 trajectory but was down-regulated along the cDC1 and DC3 trajectories ( Fig. 6I ). CD207 expression selectively increased along the putative cDC2 trajectory, whereas CD1C expression increased along both cDC2 and DC3 trajectories and decreased along the cDC1 trajectory ( Fig. 6I ). Last, the clusters associated with the putative DC3–like trajectory displayed a progressive increase in expression of the myeloid and DC3-associated genes CD163 , CD14 , S100A9 , C1QA , and MERTK as they transitioned through clusters 2B, 3B, and 8 to mature DC3s ( Fig. 6I ), albeit these genes were expressed at far lower levels than observed in the mono/mac clusters ( Fig. 1E ). We found that the early cDC2 precursor-like cluster 6, which expressed lower levels of HLA-DR and ITGAX and higher levels of KIT compared with mature cDC2s ( Fig. 6I ), also bore a transcriptional resemblance to RORγt-DC–like cells that were recently identified in human tonsil and intestine ( 58 ), including expression of RORC and the histone methyltranferase PRDM1 6 ( Fig. 6J and fig. S6D), but not to progenitors of RORγt − ILC3s (type 3 innate lymphoid cells) (fig. S6E). The bioinformatic-based trajectory and transcriptional analyses described above are inherently predictive, and more direct studies are required to determine their validity.

To investigate whether intestinal cDC proportions are altered in IBD, we performed flow cytometry on digested colonic biopsies from treatment-naïve patients, undergoing endoscopic screening for IBD diagnosis and subsequently diagnosed with CD or UC (table S2). Areas of active inflammation contained reduced proportions of CD1c + CD207 + CD11a − DCs and increased proportions of CD1c + CD207 − CD11a + DCs ( Fig. 7A ). To determine whether the increased proportion of CD1c + CD207 − CD11a + DCs represented increased numbers of DC3s or an alternative DC population, we performed CITE-seq on sorted CD45 + CD3 − CD19 − HLADR int/+ cells from ileal LP surgical samples from patients with CD with more- and less-inflamed regions taken from each patient (table S3). After bioinformatic removal of monos/macs, CD and CRC datasets were integrated and the identity of each cluster was determined using the DC labels from the CRC samples, together with DC subset–specific signature scores (fig. The proportions of cDC2s and ambiguous cells were reduced, whereas cDC1 and DC3 proportions remained similar in more-inflamed compared with less-inflamed areas of CD LPs ( Fig. 7C ). In contrast, the proportion of CCR7 + cDCs increased with inflammation, although this did not reach statistical significance ( Fig. 7C ). CCR7 + cDCs differed in their expression of >5000 genes compared with total CCR7 − DCs, including increased expression of the lysosomal gene LAMP3 , maturation markers, and the inhibitory molecules CD273 (encoding PD-L2) and CD274 (encoding PD-L1) ( Fig. 7D , fig. CCR7 + cDCs were enriched in GO terms associated with NF-κB and cytokine signaling, response to type II interferons, microvillus organization, antigen processing and presentation, and regulation of lymphocyte proliferation ( Fig. 7E ). This transcriptional profile resembles that of previously described mature regulatory (mREG) or mature CCR7 + cDCs ( 42 ). Consistent with previous findings ( 59 , 60 ), CCR7 + LAMP + cDCs were readily detected in inflamed LPs of patients with CD and in intimate association with T cell aggregates, some of which were proliferating ( Fig. 7F ).
CCR7 + cDCs split into two main subclusters that differed in expression of 4924 genes (fig. S7, E and F, and data file S7). Of these, CCR7 + cDC subcluster 1 was enriched in GO terms such as aerobic respiration, defense against virus, phagocytosis, and antigen processing, whereas subcluster 2 was enriched in GO terms associated with NF-κB and Wnt signaling, T cell activation, and proliferation (fig. CCR7 + cDC subcluster 1 directly connected with the cDC2 and ambiguous clusters, whereas CCR7 + subcluster 2 connected only with subcluster 1 ( Fig. 7C and movie S2), suggesting that cells in subcluster 2 arose from subcluster 1. These results are consistent with recent findings that CCR7 + cDCs in the synovial tissues of patients with rheumatoid arthritis consist of MIR155 + cDCs and LAMP3 + cDCs that originate from cDC2s, with LAMP3 + cDCs appearing to arise from MIR155 + cDC intermediates ( 61 ). The signature score of synovial-derived MIR155 + cDCs correlated most closely with CCR7 + cDC subcluster 1, whereas that of LAMP3 + cDCs correlated most closely with subcluster 2 (fig. S7H).
Each DC subset exhibited few DEGs between more- and less-inflamed LP regions, with IFITM1 - IFITM3 , that encode interferon-induced transmembrane proteins, up-regulated in cDC2s, ambiguous cells, and DC3s in more-inflamed LPs ( Fig. 7H and data file S8). Moreover, CCR7 + subcluster 1 was highly enriched in interferon response genes ( Fig. 7I ). Collectively, these bioinformatic-based analyses suggest that cDC2s, and potentially DC3s, differentiate in the LPs of patients with CD into CCR7 + cDCs, that this differentiation is associated with a transient increase in interferon signaling, and that a proportion of these cells are maintained in a T cell–rich LP niche.
DISCUSSION
Characterizing MNP diversity in distinct human tissues is essential for our understanding of their roles in homeostasis and disease. Here, we extend previous studies of human intestinal MNPs ( 18 , 37 , 60 , 62 , 63 ), demonstrating that the human ileal and colonic LPs contain numerous transcriptionally distinct MNPs, including monocytes, mono/mac intermediates, differentiated macrophage subsets, cDC1s, cDC2s, DC3s, mature CCR7 + cDCs, and lineage-specific cDC precursor–like cells. We further show that the relative proportions of many of these populations change across sites and in the setting of inflammation. Collectively, our results provide a roadmap of the human intestinal MNP compartment and a framework for future studies aimed at modulating this compartment for therapeutic purposes.
Prior scRNA-seq analyses of human intestinal macrophages have focused primarily on the colon and demonstrated macrophage diversity in both the mucosa and underlying mucosa muscularis ( 18 , 19 , 64 ). Here, we confirm and extend these findings by showing that the ileal and colonic LPs contain similar populations of monocytes, intermediates and differentiated macrophage subsets and/or states albeit in different proportions. Although our trajectory analysis suggested that the major intestinal macrophage subsets derive from blood monocytes, intestinal myeloid populations change with time ( 65 ), and it remains possible that macrophage populations of embryonic origin exist in the LPs of younger individuals and that the rate of replenishment may vary across subsets and life course. Our findings further suggest that the major differentiated macrophage subsets develop in situ via distinct monocyte-derived intermediates. Such results are in line with murine studies demonstrating that monocyte-derived macrophage subsets acquire specialized functions in response to local niche-specific signals as they differentiate toward tissue residency ( 66 ).
Although the mechanisms driving differences in the relative proportions of mono/mac subsets between the ileum and colon remain unclear, we speculate that the high proportions of intermediate monocytes in the colonic LP may reflect higher turnover of this compartment. Given that different populations of mature macrophages occupy distinct anatomical niches in tissues, including around blood vessels and neurons ( 67 ), differences in the proportion of macrophage subsets between the ileum and colon may reflect variation in the sizes of these niches in the two tissues. This may also help explain why we observed limited differences in the transcriptional profiles of differentiated macrophage subsets between the ileum and colon because the local factors specifying the final fate of each macrophage subset may be shared between these sites. Our flow cytometry analysis also confirmed and extended prior studies ( 60 , 62 , 68 ), showing that the proportions of both early and late intermediate cells increased, whereas the proportions of differentiated macrophages decreased, in IBD and that these alterations correlated with disease severity. Whether intermediate cells accumulating in IBD are transcriptionally similar to those present in the healthy intestine or, as suggested in mice ( 69 ), acquire a distinct transcriptional profile because of local inflammatory cues, requires further analysis.
The intestinal LP compartment contained cDC1s and cDC2s as well as DCs that expressed both cDC2- and mono/mac-associated genes, which, in line with previous studies ( 33 , 70 ), we termed DC3s. Our trajectory analysis suggested phenotypic convergence between cDC2s and DC3s in the intestinal LP, and we were unable to distinguish some of these cells transcriptionally and using surface markers. However, our CITE-seq analysis demonstrated that CD207 + CD11a − CD1c + cells were highly enriched in cDC2s, whereas CD207 − CD11a + CD1c + cells were enriched in DC3s in the healthy intestine. Using these markers, we found that DC3s were present in higher proportions in the colonic compared with the ileal LPs, whereas cDC2s showed the opposite pattern. Intestinal DC3s displayed a distinct proinflammatory transcriptional profile compared with other cDCs and differentiated macrophage subsets, suggesting that they may play distinct roles in intestinal immunity. Recent lineage tracing and transcriptional analysis has identified additional complexity in the murine cDC2 compartment, including cDC2A and cDC2B that appear to arise from distinct precursors ( 71 – 74 ). The relationship between these populations and the human intestinal cDC2/DC3-like cells described here remains to be assessed.
Whereas lineage-restricted cDC precursors have been identified in human blood, bone marrow, and lymphoid tissues ( 25 , 29 , 40 , 50 – 52 ), their existence in the human intestine has remained unclear. Here, our combined bioinformatic analyses indicated that both the human ileal and colonic LPs may contain precursors that are committed to either the cDC1, cDC2, or DC3 lineage. First, we found that each mature cDC subset was directly connected in trajectory space to a distinct population of proliferating HLA low ITGAX low cells. Second, these populations displayed a unidirectional velocity-based developmental trajectory into either mature cDC1s, cDC2s, or DC3s. Third, these putative lineage-restricted precursors displayed a progressive acquisition or loss of cDC lineage–associated marker genes and TFs as they transitioned toward each mature cDC subset. As expected, the number of these putative lineage-restricted cDC precursors was low, and our ability to capture such cells was likely enhanced by our sorting strategy and use of surgical resection material. This bioinformatic-based evidence of lineage-restricted DC precursors in the human intestinal LP is consistent with recent studies indicating the presence of cDC1- and cDC2-restricted cDC precursors in the murine small intestine ( 53 ) and that mouse and human DC3s derive from distinct precursors to those of cDC1s and cDC2s ( 27 , 29 , 40 , 74 , 75 ). Direct studies will be required to determine the validity of these conclusions.
Our observation that the putative cDC2-like precursor cluster 6 bore a transcriptional resemblance to PRDM1 + RORγt DC-like cells, identified in human tonsil and intestine ( 58 ), suggests the intriguing possibility that some of these cells may have the potential of giving rise to cDC2s. Those authors made a similar suggestion on the basis of their findings that some PRDM1 + RORγt DC–like cells lay directly upstream of cDC2s in trajectory analysis, which they classified as DC progenitors when mapping to the human fetal tissue atlas and from in vitro cultures ( 58 ). Further studies will be required to directly assess this possibility, as well as any potential link between these cells and the murine RORγt + antigen-presenting cells that play a key role in peripheral regulatory T cell induction to microbial and food antigens ( 76 – 81 ). In addition to putative lineage-restricted DC precursors, we also identified a minor population of proliferating ITGAX -expressing HLA low cells whose transcriptional profile correlated best with early bone marrow precursors, indicating that these cells may lie upstream of lineage-committed cDC precursors. Although such findings are consistent with the observation that hematopoietic stem cells and/or downstream myeloid precursors are present in the human intestine ( 82 ), their role in maintaining the intestinal MNP compartment remains to be determined.
In the inflamed LPs of patients with CD, we found that the proportion of cDC2s was reduced, whereas that of CCR7 + cDCs was increased. These CCR7 + cDCs bore a similar transcriptional profile to migratory and mREG cDCs, a population present in diverse contexts including tumors and rheumatoid synovium ( 26 , 42 , 61 , 83 ). Whereas CCR7 induction is usually associated with cDC migration to draining lymph nodes, clusters of LAMP3 + CCR7 + cDCs were readily detected in the inflamed LPs of patients with CD in intimate association with T cell aggregates, indicating a potential role in regulating local T cell responses. Previous studies reported CCR7 + cDCs in the inflamed LPs of a subset of patients with IBD associated with resistance to anti-TNF therapy ( 59 ). Whereas tumor-associated CCR7 + cDCs derive from cDC1s and cDC2s, our trajectory and CITE-seq analysis suggested that the CCR7 + cDCs accumulating in inflamed LPs of patients with CD originated from cDC2s and potentially DC3s, but not from cDC1s. Furthermore, we found two developmentally related CCR7 + cDC subclusters, with a more inflammatory CCR7 + cDC cluster giving rise to a CCR7 + cDCs enriched in T cell activation pathways. Similarly, in the synovial tissues of patients with rheumatoid arthritis, cDC2-derived CCR7 + cDCs were found associated with lymphoid-like clusters ( 61 ), indicating a common immunological niche that develops in both diseases.
In summary, our single-cell data highlight marked heterogeneity in the MNP compartment of the human intestinal LP, varying along the length of the human intestine and in the setting of disease. By identifying transcriptomic and phenotypic markers, our work provides a road map for the study of intestinal MNP subsets and their contribution to intestinal immune responses in health and disease.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study design
The main objectives of this study were to determine (i) MNP heterogeneity within the human intestinal LP, (ii) whether precursor-like to mature MNP subsets were present in the intestine, (iii) whether the proportions and transcriptional profiles of mature MNP subsets differed between human ileum and colon, and (iv) inflammation-induced changes in the intestinal MNP compartment. Our hypothesis was that multimodal single-cell methods (scRNA-seq, CITE-seq, and flow cytometry) combined with bioinformatic analysis would allow for the unambiguous identification of MNP subsets within the intestine. Patient material included tissue from surgical specimens from (i) patients with CRC (>10 cm from tumor site), (ii) patients with CD undergoing surgery for disease relief, and (iii) biopsies from treatment-naïve patients undergoing endoscopy for suspected IBD. Patients below 18 or above 85 years of age were excluded from the study. Surgical samples were only used when it was possible to readily dissect mucosa from SM. Each experiment was replicated in at least three patients unless otherwise specified. No sampling replication was performed within an individual patient due to limited tissue availability.
Human participants
Resection samples were obtained from patients undergoing surgery for CRC and from patients with CD without comorbidities such as infectious or neoplastic diseases undergoing surgery for disease relief (see tables S1 and S3 for further patient information), after informed consent with ethical approval from the Scientific Ethics Committee of the Copenhagen Capital Region, Denmark (H-3-2013-118 and H-20054066). Biopsy samples were obtained from adult patients at the time of their first colonoscopy, during which the diagnosis of IBD was established (both CD and UC), or for ongoing disease assessment (see table S2 for further patient information) at the Western General Hospital, Edinburgh, UK, after informed consent under existing approvals (REC:19/ES/0087). All patients were part of the Lothian IBD registry ( 84 ) and a diagnosis of IBD was made using the Lennard-Jones criteria ( 85 ). Endoscopic assessment of disease severity at each biopsy site was made at the time of endoscopy [Simple Endoscopic Score for CD ( 86 ), or Mayo endoscopic subscore ( 87 )], and biopsy sites were classified as quiescent, mild, moderate, or severe based on the above scoring (See table S2 for more information). Two to four biopsies were taken per site and pooled for analysis. Further clinical information for biopsy samples were collected under the above approvals (REC:19/ES/0087) and included blood parameters (complement-reactive protein, hemoglobin, and albumin), stool biomarkers (fecal calprotectin), and drug treatment that were obtained ±2 weeks of the date of endoscopy (see table S2).
Tissue processing
Surgical samples were processed as described previously ( 23 ). Muscularis externa was removed using curved surgical scissors, and the remaining tissue was incubated in RPMI-5 [RPMI, 5% fetal calf serum (FCS), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin] containing 4 mM dithiothreitol for 2 × 10 min at 37°C on a shaking incubator (370 rpm) to remove mucus. Macroscopically visible SM was trimmed away using scissors and mucosa separated from SM under a stereo microscope using forceps. Epithelial cells were removed by incubating the mucosa in Ca2 + and Mg2 + -free Hanks’ balanced salt solution containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 5 mM EDTA at 37°C for 10 min in a shaking incubator, and this procedure was repeated four times. Isolated lymphoid follicles were dissected from the mucosa using a scalpel under a stereo microscope with a transmitted light source, and the remaining GALT-free LP was cut into 2- to 4-mm 2 pieces in preparation for digestion. LP was incubated in RPMI-5 containing deoxyribonuclease 1 (30 μg/ml) and collagenase D (5 mg/ml) or Liberase TM (2.5 mg/ml) for 45 min at 37°C under gentle shaking (370 rpm). The resulting LP cell suspension was passed through a 100-μm filter and washed twice in fresh RPMI for downstream analysis. Biopsy samples were processed using the same protocol without peeling away the SM but with any visible follicles removed.
Flow cytometry, MNP enrichment, and cell sorting
Cell suspensions were stained with indicated antibodies in Brilliant stain buffer (BD Biosciences) containing 4% normal mouse serum, according to standard procedures, with dead cells identified by 7-AAD staining and excluded from analysis. Samples were analyzed on an LSR Fortessa 2 (BD Biosciences) using Flowjo software (BD). The Legendscreen assay (BioLegend) was performed as per the manufacturer’s instructions. For scRNA-seq experiments, LP cell suspensions were either enriched for HLA-DR + cells using anti–HLA-DR microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) and LS magnetic-activated cell sorting columns according to the manufacturer’s instructions or processed further as total single-cell suspensions without enrichment. The resulting cells were stained with the indicated antibodies (table S4) and 7-AAD or LIVE/DEAD Fixable Far Red Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to exclude dead cells. Cells were sorted on a FACSMelody or FACSAria Fusion (BD) for subsequent scRNA-seq. For CITE-seq analysis, the cells were stained with barcode-labeled TotalSeq-A antibodies (table S4) or TotalSeq-C Human Universal Cocktail V1.0 (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions before sorting.
Immunohistochemical analysis
Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned (4 μm) and deparaffinized. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating slides in IHC-Tek Epitope Retrieval Solution (IHC World) for 30 min in a steamer. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies (table S4) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in staining buffer [phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing FCS (2%), Tween 20 (0.1%), and ProClin 200 (0.05%)] overnight, washed in PBS containing Tween 20 (0.2%) for >4 hours, and then with secondary antibodies (table S4) overnight in staining buffer. After washing, the slides were imaged using an LSM900 confocal laser microscope equipped with a two-channel gallium arsenide phosphide photomultiplier tube; 405-nm (5 mW), 488-nm (10 mW), 561-nm (10 mW), and 639-nm (5 mW) diode lasers; a Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 objective; and Zen Blue v3.5 software (Zeiss). Images were processed with Imaris Viewer v10.1.1 for adjusting pseudocolor (Oxford Instruments).
10x chromium and sequencing
Freshly isolated sorted single cells were subjected to droplet-based massively parallel scRNA-seq using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v2, the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 with Feature Barcoding technology for Cell Surface Protein or the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kits v2 (Dual Index) with Feature Barcode technology for Cell Surface Protein and Immune Receptor Mapping (10x Genomics) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For CITE-seq analysis, 5 μl of the purified smaller cDNA product was used as template for antibody-derived tag (ADT) sequencing and ADT sequencing libraries were constructed and indexed following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quality and quantity of the final libraries were measured using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer equipped with High Sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent). Illumina sequencing was carried out at the Genomics Core Unit, Center of Excellence for Fluorescent Bioanalytics (University of Regensburg, Germany), at the SNP&SEQ Technology Platform, Sweden or at the Core Facility for Flow Cytometry and Single Cell Analysis, Faculty of Health and Medical Sciences, University of Copenhagen. Libraries were sequenced using HiSeq, NextSeq, and NovaSeq systems (300 cycles), aiming for a minimum of 30,000 read pairs per cell for sc-RNA and 3000 read pairs per cell for ADTs.
Bioinformatic analysis
Data processing
Sequencing data were preprocessed and aligned with 10x Genomics Cell Ranger (version 2.2.0, 3.1.0, and 8.0.0) ( 88 , 89 ). Sequencing data from samples stained with TotalSeq antibodies were processed with CITE-seq count ( 90 ). Each sample was read into a Seurat (version 3.1.5/5.0.1/5.2.1) ( 91 ) object in R (versions 3.5.1/4.0.1/4.3.0/4.3.1) ( 92 ) and processed by removing cells with exceptionally low or high unique molecular identifiers (UMI), gene counts (<500 to 1000 and >3000 to 6000 genes per cell), and mitochondrial gene content (>10%) according to current best practice ( 93 ) and likely representing debris and doublets. For CD samples, we predicted and removed doublets using scDblFinder (version 1.16.0) ( 94 ). To normalize CITE-seq data by denoising and scaling protein levels against background [denoised and scaled by background (DSB) normalization ( 95 )], the debris removed from each sample was used as empty droplet information (free floating CITE-seq antibody), whereas isotype controls were used to normalize for nonspecific binding ( 95 ). The normalized protein data were incorporated with the RNA data for the individual samples by adding them to the corresponding Seurat objects.
After log-normalization of RNA levels for individual samples, cell cycle gene modules were calculated using the Seurat CellCycleScoring function and variable genes were identified per sample. After initial data processing, all the samples were integrated either with Seurat anchor integration or harmony ( 96 ). Gene expression was subsequently scaled, regressing out the effect of cell cycle, UMI counts, and mitochondrial gene content based on their scoring on the individual samples except when combining data in Fig. 7 .
Identification of MNP subsets
To detect MNP subsets, a high-resolution clustering was initially performed, which on the basis of known monocyte-, macrophage-, and cDC2-associated genes split the clusters into three main groups. To validate these, we performed a PCA on the average expression of marker genes for subsets (“Macro,” “cMo,” “DC2/3,” and “mReg”) published by Mulder et al. ( 35 ).
Differential gene expression and gene ontology
Cluster markers were computed using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function. Differential gene expression tests between specific cell subsets and across conditions were performed by pseudobulk analysis with DESeq2 (version 1.38.3/1.42.1) ( 97 ) using Seurat’s AggregateExpression function. GO analysis was performed on the basis of DEG lists of above ( P adj <0.05) and run on EnrichR’s web interface ( 98 ). The output tables based on GO Biological Processes 2023/2025 were downloaded and plotted in R using the package ggradar (version 0.1).
Comparisons with public datasets
Module scores were calculated with Seurat’s AddModuleScore function on indicated selected gene sets or gene sets from indicated literature. For analysis of the datasets from Triana et al. ( 56 ), the processed Seurat object was downloaded and subsetted on the basis of the clusters labeled “HSCs and MPPs,” “lymphomyeloid prog,” “early promyelocytes,” “cDC1,” “cDC2,” “late promyelocytes,” “myelocytes,” and “classical monocytes.” Gene expression data were averaged and Pearson correlations calculated on the basis of variable genes from our data also present in the bone marrow dataset.
Signaling pathway analysis
DoRothEA was run using confidence levels A + B and referring to the human DoRothEA TF interaction database ( 48 ). Progeny was run using organism = human and top = 500 genes ( 49 ).
Trajectory inference
Trajectory inference was performed with tSpace on PC spaces of indicated populations ( 36 ). The outputs were dimensionality reduced with UMAP ( 99 , 100 ) from tPCs 1-15 (for both cDCs and macrophages), to 2D and 3D tUMAPs with distance metric set to Pearson. 3D tUMAP for the tSpace trajectories were angled and embedded into 2D. For CRC samples, clustering was performed with Louvain clustering for Seurat on the tPCs as input. Pseudotime (arbitrary timescale unit) was calculated by taking all trajectories from the tSpace output from M1 and averaging these per cell. Splicing patterns were first determined on individual samples with the advanced run setting for velocyto ( 55 ) with a repeated annotation file ( 101 ). Genes were filtered by 0.2 for spliced data and 0.05 for unspliced data. RNA velocity estimates were then calculated for T = 1 and only included genes with splicing information also present in the variable genes and only on cells of interest (e.g., precursors) ( 102 ). The information was embedded on top of 2D tUMAP using n = 400, scale = sqrt, grid. n = 50 and arrow.scale = 2.
Quantification and statistical analysis
Statistical analysis of flow cytometry data was performed using Prism software (GraphPad). Statistical analysis of sequencing data was performed in R. Statistical tests used for experimental data are outlined in the figure legends.
