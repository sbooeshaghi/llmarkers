SUMMARY
Clear cell renal cell carcinomas (ccRCCs) are highly immune infiltrated, but the effect of immune heterogeneity on clinical outcome in ccRCC has not been fully characterized. Here we perform paired single-cell RNA (scRNA) and T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing of 167,283 cells from multiple tumor regions, lymph node, normal kidney, and peripheral blood of two immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-naïve and four ICB-treated patients to map the ccRCC immune landscape. We detect extensive heterogeneity within and between patients, with enrichment of CD8A + tissue-resident T cells in a patient responsive to ICB and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in a resistant patient. A TCR trajectory framework suggests distinct T cell differentiation pathways between patients responding and resistant to ICB. Finally, scRNA-derived signatures of tissue-resident T cells and TAMs are associated with response to ICB and targeted therapies across multiple independent cohorts. Our study establishes a multimodal interrogation of the cellular programs underlying therapeutic efficacy in ccRCC.

In Brief
Krishna et al. dissect the immune microenvironment and TCR clonotype dynamics in multiregional clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) samples at single cell resolution. They show that CD8 + tissue-resident T cells and distinct tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) populations are associated with response and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in ccRCC.
INTRODUCTION
Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy and combination regimens have significantly increased survival in patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most common and lethal form of RCC( Motzer et al., 2015 , 2018 , 2019a ; Rini et al., 2019a ). However, prediction of which patients will respond to therapy remains a fundamental problem. Studies have shown that prior genomic correlates of ICB response in solid tumors—i.e. tumor mutation burden (TMB) and PD-L1 status( Havel et al., 2019 ; Riaz et al., 2017 ; Snyder et al., 2014 )—are not predictive in ccRCC( McDermott et al., 2018 ; Miao et al., 2018 ; Şenbabaoğlu et al., 2016 ), which may suggest a role for the immune microenvironment in mediating clinical benefit. Accordingly, we and others have demonstrated that ccRCC is among the most immune and vascularly infiltrated cancer types( Hegde and Chen, 2020 ; Rooney et al., 2015 ; Şenbabaoğlu et al., 2016 ; Thompson et al., 2007 ; Yoshihara et al., 2013 ). Prior genomic characterization of ccRCC has focused on ICB-naïve patients profiled via bulk RNA-sequencing( Şenbabaoğlu et al., 2016 ) or CYTOF( Chevrier et al., 2017 ), which are limited by resolution. Thus, a complete account of the ccRCC immune microenvironment is required for more accurate predictions of patient response and would inform the development of new targeted therapies.
Prior transcriptomic studies have largely focused on single-region tumor biopsies, undersampling variation in immune infiltration within patients ( Zhang et al., 2018 ). Here, we utilize paired single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing to dissect the immune microenvironment in both ICB-naïve and treated ccRCC patients in an unbiased, comprehensive manner. To quantify the extent of within-patient diversity in immune infiltration, we performed multiregional and multi-tissue sampling for each patient.
RESULTS
Patient cohorts and study design
To resolve the architecture of the immune microenvironment of ccRCC, we first selected a cohort of six patients with advanced disease as recently profiled in other large scale genomics analyses ( Turajlic et al., 2018a , 2018b ), spanning a variety of treatments and clinical responses ( Figures 1A , 1B , and S1A ). Two patients in our study underwent single cell profiling prior to ICB administration, and 4 received either anti-PD1 monotherapy (Nivolumab, hereafter Nivo) or anti-CTLA4 (Ipilimumab, hereafter Ipi) + anti-PD1. The two patients designated as untreated in our study (Untreated 1 and Untreated 2) did not receive any treatment before single cell profiling, per Figure 1A . 3 out of the 4 patients designated as treated (Nivo-exposed, Ipi/Nivo resistant, Ipi/Nivo mixed response) did not receive any additional treatment prior to single cell profiling. The 4 th treated patient (Ipi/Nivo complete response) progressed on Sunitinib therapy, then responded to Ipi/Nivo therapy before single cell profiling.

We generated single cell transcriptomes from tumor, lymph node, adjacent normal kidney, and PBMCs using droplet-based 5’ single-cell RNA sequencing and T cell receptor sequencing ( Figure S1B – E ). Moreover, we profiled three tumor regions per patient—one directly adjacent to normal kidney (hereafter ‘Near’), one distal (‘Far’), and one in between (‘Center’) (n = 29 samples total) ( Figure 1C ). To link immune phenotypes discovered via single cell transcriptomics to histologic tumor regression and tumor morphologic classification, tumor samples also underwent pathologic review ( Figure 1D – F ).
Single cell transcriptional landscape of ICB-naïve and treated ccRCCs
We first merged and normalized all 29 samples to create a reference cell atlas across patients, correcting for batch effects between transcriptional profiles from each patient by matching mutual nearest neighbors( Haghverdi et al., 2018 ) prior to principal component analysis (PCA) and visualization via uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). This analysis demonstrated clustering of scRNA samples broadly by tissue, not patient, suggesting successful mitigation of batch effects ( Figure S2A ). In total, we obtained scRNA profiles from 167283 cells and TCR clonotypes from 43828 cells. Louvain clustering( Levine et al., 2015 ; Xu and Su, 2015 ) revealed 28 clusters across tissues spanning lymphoid, myeloid, and epithelial cells, as well as cancer cells ( Figures 2A – C , S2B and Table S1 ).

We detected 5 CD8 + T cell clusters ( Figure 2D ), with three characterized by expression of activation and exhaustion markers PDCD1 , LAG3 , HAVCR2 , and TOX among others ( Figure S2C ). Differential expression analyses ( Figures 2E and S2C ) revealed that one of these clusters (“CD8A + Exhausted”) expressed particularly high levels of exhaustion markers, while another (“CD8A + Proliferating”) expressed genes associated with cell cycling and proliferation, e.g. MKI67 , TOP2A , and STMN1 . The third (“CD8A + Exhausted Immediate Early Genes (IEG)”) was distinguished by expression of genes induced early after activation, e.g. JUNB , FOS , ATF3 , HSPA1A , and DNAJB1 ( Table S1 ), perhaps indicative of recent TCR stimulation. This third cluster also demonstrated high expression of the transcription factor NR4A1 , previously linked to T cell dysfunction( Liu et al., 2019 ).
We detected two additional CD8A + T cell clusters with non-exhausted phenotypes. One expressed the tissue-residency markers CD69 and ZNF683 and was termed CD8A + tissue-resident. Cells in this cluster were CD49A low and CD103 + and expressed PDCD1 and LAG3 ( Figure S2C ). We also found expression of the effector molecules IFNG , GZMH , PRF1 , and KLRG1 on these cells as well as CD44 , a hallmark of tissue-resident memory cells( Topham and Reilly, 2018 ) ( Figure S2D ). Finally, we observed a CD3D + CD8 + T cell cluster marked by high expression of genes typically associated with NK cell activity, e.g. KLRB1 , FCGR3A , GZMH , and GNLY . This cluster was thus termed “NK-like T cell”.
Within the CD4 + T cell compartment, we detected five clusters. Two displayed transcriptional profiles that mirrored those of the CD8 + proliferating and CD8 + exhausted IEG clusters and were thus named CD4 + proliferating and CD4 + activated IEG ( Figures 2E and S2E ). We identified a cluster of regulatory T cells (“CD4 + Treg”) characterized by expression of the transcription factor FOXP3 , as well BATF , CTLA4 , and TIGIT . Another (“CD4 + effector”) was distinguished by expression of cytotoxic markers GZMA , GZMK , and NKG7 . Finally, we observed a CD4 + naïve cluster primarily restricted to the blood, expressing high levels of TCF7 , CCR7 , and SELL . Notably, TCF7 was here expressed on CD4 + , not CD8 + T cells, consistent with a recent report describing TCF7 expression on CD4 + T cells in patients with lung cancer ( Ghorani et al., 2020 ).
We next sought to phenotype the myeloid compartment in ccRCC ( Figure 2F ). We observed two monocyte clusters ( Figure S3A )—one primarily restricted to the blood ( CD14 + Monocyte) and another CD14 + / 16 + population characteristic of tissue-resident monocytes( Ginhoux and Jung, 2014 ). DC populations segregated into three well-defined clusters ( Figure S3B )—cDC1, cDC2, and plasmacytoid (pDC)( Merad et al., 2013 ).
The phenotypic diversity of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) is of great interest in ccRCC, given their involvement in angiogenic pathways supporting tumor growth( DeNardo et al., 2010 ; Hanahan and Coussens, 2012 ; Qian and Pollard, 2010 ; Wynn et al., 2013 ) and association with tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) outcome( Hakimi et al., 2019 ). Our data pointed to the existence of 4 TAM clusters, generally characterized by a spectrum of HLA-DR expression. The first (“TAM HLA hi ”) demonstrated a classically immunosuppressive phenotype, with the highest expression of HLA-DR among all TAM clusters, complement genes (e.g. C1QA/B ), APOE , and TREM2 ( Figures 2G and Figure S3C ). The second demonstrated intermediate expression of HLA-DR (“TAM HLA int ”), and also expressed the immunosuppressive CD163 as well as CSF1R and heat shock proteins ( Figure S3C ) ( Table S1 ). We also observed expression of IL1A , SIGLEC1 ( CD169 ) and MRC1 ( CD206 ) in this cluster ( Figures S3D – F ), consistent with the idea that TAMs express combinations of both pro- and anti-inflammatory markers( Grabowska et al., 2018 ). The third and fourth clusters (“TAM ISG hi ” and “TAM ISG int ) were defined by low expression of HLA-DR and varying levels of interferon signaling (ISG) genes, i.e. IFITM1 , IFITM3 , and IL1B ( Figures 2G and S3C ). Interestingly, TAM ISG hi and ISG int also expressed markers of genes associated with angiogenesis, e.g. FLT1 , SPARC , and RGS5 ( Arnold et al., 2014 ; Kzhyshkowska et al., 2006 ; Qian et al., 2015 ).
We next investigated the relationship between intratumoral immune populations by performing a correlation analysis across all tumor samples (n = 18) from our single cell cohort. We observed positive correlations between CD8 + populations (CD8A + Exhausted vs CD8A + Proliferating, rho = 0.72, FDR P = 0.03), between CD8 + and CD4 + populations (CD8A + Exhausted vs CD4 + Treg, rho = 0.85, FDR P < 2.e-16), and between TAM populations (TAM HLA hi vs TAM HLA int , rho = 0.81, FDR P < 0.006), suggesting that these populations may act in concert to promote pro- or anti-tumor immunity ( Figures 2H and S4 ). Intriguingly, we observed a positive correlation between the CD8A + Exhausted IEG population and cDC1 (rho = 0.72, FDR P = 0.02), potentially suggesting that CD8A + Exhausted IEG cells may encounter antigen through cross-presentation by cDC1( Theisen and Murphy, 2017 ). However, further functional experiments in large cohorts will be required to validate these associations.
Multiregional immune heterogeneity in ccRCC
We next sought to quantify variation in the prevalence of these populations within and across patients. For each patient sample, we computed the percentage of each immune cluster identified via scRNA analysis (n = 29) out of all immune clusters ( Figure 3A ). We detected striking within-patient immune diversity, with some tumor regions characterized by extensive T cell infiltration and others characterized by T-cell excluded, TAM-infiltrated phenotypes. Pathologic review indicated a link between region-specific immune phenotypes and tumor characteristics ( Figures 3B and S5 ). For example, the Untreated 1 Center region—heavily infiltrated with CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, and NK cells—demonstrated complete tumor regression, whereas the Untreated 2 Far region—characterized by high prevalence of the TAM HLA hi and HLA int populations—was predominantly high-grade, with no tumor regression.

This analysis provided insight into specific immune populations that may drive ICB response. The Ipi/Nivo resistant patient was primarily T-cell excluded and demonstrated the highest prevalence of TAM populations of all patients studied, suggesting that TAM HLA hi and HLA int —may promote ICB resistance via T cell exclusion. Notably, the lymph node metastasis from this patient was completely devoid of infiltrating lymphocytes and extensively infiltrated with TAMs ( Figures 3A , 3B ). Strikingly, in the patient with complete response to ICB we observed strong enrichment of the CD8A + tissue-resident cluster across all tumor regions as well as low TAM infiltration. These data, coupled with H&E images that demonstrated complete tumor regression across regions from this patient ( Figures 3B and S5P – R ), as well as the relative absence of conventionally exhausted CD8 + T cells (i.e. CD8A + Exhausted, Proliferating, and Exhausted IEG), may suggest that CD8A + tissue-resident cells are activated after ICB administration.
We next examined the expression of the Javelin and T-effector signatures, previously shown to be predictive of ICB response( Choueiri et al., 2019 ), across cells from all scRNA samples ( Figures 3C and S6A ) and detected extensive variability across tumor regions and adjacent tissues. Though this analysis is conducted in a small multiregional cohort of only six patients, these data raise two important points. First, bulk RNA-seq-derived signatures are vulnerable to tumor region sampling bias, consistent with a recent report examining the effect of intratumor heterogeneity on prognostic signature expression in NSCLC( Biswas et al., 2019 ). Second, signature expression can be captured in adjacent normal kidney and PBMCs, in some cases exceeding intratumoral expression. To further validate these findings, we analyzed a cohort of 29 patients with bulk RNA-sequencing of multiregional tumor samples using scRNA-derived signatures (Methods) and detected pronounced intratumoral immune heterogeneity across ICB-naïve, ICB-treated, and ICB+TKI-treated patients ( Figure 3D ). Importantly, we found that in both pre- and post-therapy samples profiled by bulk RNA-seq, the TAM ISG hi population was positively correlated with an angiogenesis signature( McDermott et al., 2018 ) ( Figures S6B and S6C ). These data provide a cellular context for prior work, which has suggested a link between angiogenesis and improved response to TKIs ( Hakimi et al., 2019 ). Furthermore, consistent with our scRNA analysis ( Figure 3A ), we detected enrichment of a CD8 + tissue-resident signature in ICB-treated patients compared to ICB-naïve patients ( Figure S6D ). Intriguingly, our analysis of multiregional normal kidney samples suggested that immune infiltration in normal kidneys varies according to sampling distance from the tumor ( Figure 3E ). These data reveal drastic within-patient immune diversity across ccRCC tumors and normal adjacent tissues and highlight the effect of this heterogeneity on the expression of clinical signatures.
Spatial and phenotypic diversity of expanded TCR clonotypes
The expansion of intratumoral TCR clonotypes upon antigen encounter is a central component of the anti-tumor immune response. Analysis of the top 50 expanded intratumoral TCR clonotypes in each patient ( Figures 4A ) revealed that in general, expanded clonotypes were derived from the exhausted T cell compartment, i.e. the CD8A + Exhausted, Exhausted IEG, and Proliferating clusters.

Intriguingly, in both the Ipi/Nivo resistant and complete response patients, the most expanded clonotype was derived not from the exhausted pool but rather from the CD8A+ tissue-resident cluster. These data, together with our scRNA ( Figure 3A ) and bulk RNA-seq analyses ( Figure S6D ), suggest that activation and expansion of CD8A + tissue-resident T cells occur after ICB administration. That conventionally exhausted T cells were not expanded in these two patients suggests that ICB administration in RCC may mobilize T cells in alternative states. The fact that such a pronounced expansion of tissue-resident T cells was not observed in the Nivo-exposed patient may reflect that this patient was treated on a very short treatment course (hence “exposed”), or further, that anti-PD1 monotherapy may act on different subsets of T cells as suggested by Wei et al ( Wei et al., 2017 ). In addition, the lack of tissue-resident expansion in the mixed response patient may be a consequence of the sarcomatoid features observed in this patient’s tumor.
We next investigated the extent to which expanded TCR clonotypes are shared across tumor regions ( Figure S7A ). This analysis indicated that in general expanded intratumoral clones are common to all tumor regions, with some exceptions. For example, expanded clones in the Untreated 2 Far region differ from those in Center and Near, suggesting local antigen encounter. Moreover, expanded intratumoral clones were detectable in PBMCs and normal kidney as described in recent reports( Wu et al., 2020 ; Yost et al., 2019 ). To validate this finding, we applied the TRUST algorithm ( Li et al., 2016 , 2017 ) to infer TCR clonotypes from our independent cohort of ccRCC patients receiving bulk RNA-sequencing. We found that normal kidney samples from many additional patients in this cohort contained expanded intratumoral clones ( Figure S7B ). The idea that expanded intratumoral clones are detectable in peripheral tissues raises the intriguing possibility of using healthy adjacent tissues to monitor anti-tumor immune and ICB responses.
The lineage commitment and antigen specificity of a T cell are based in part on its TCR clonotype sequence ( Carter et al., 2019 ; de Greef et al., 2020 ; Venturi et al., 2011 ; Wang and Reinherz, 2012 ). Accordingly, we sought to use paired TCR αβ sequences to resolve potential differentiation trajectories between T cell subsets, analogous to a recent ‘CellTagging’ study using combinatorial cell indexing to trace lineage and clonal history ( Biddy et al., 2018 ). We focused on CD8 + T cells, since clones from these cells were particularly expanded in all patients.
Given the expansion of tissue-resident T cells observed in the patient with a complete response to ICB, we first asked whether tissue-resident cells could be linked by differentiation trajectories to other cell states, and whether these states differed between patients responding or resistant to therapy. We adapted the trajectory analysis method Palantir ( Setty et al., 2019 ) to incorporate only cells sharing paired TCR αβ sequences with cells from the tissue-resident cluster (5050 cells total, Figure 4B ), while simultaneously controlling for batch effects (Methods, Figure 4C ). This framework was termed ‘TCR trajectory analysis’. A pseudotime ordering of cells revealed two potential trajectories from the tissue-resident cluster—one towards exhaustion, and one towards NK-like T cells ( Figure 4D ). These data may reflect the capacity of intratumoral tissue-resident cells to become exhausted ( Boldison et al., 2014 ; Hartana et al., 2018 ), in addition to shared transcriptional programs with NK-like T cells, in line with prior work characterizing the tissue-resident properties of innate-like lymphocytes ( Dadi et al., 2016 ).
We next asked whether these trajectories differed between the patient with a complete response to ICB and the patient resistant to therapy. Notably, our TCR trajectory analysis suggested a transition between tissue-resident T cells and NK-like T cells, whereas tissue-resident T cells from the resistant patient were more “fixed”, with no trajectory towards either of the other two clusters sharing TCRs with the starting cluster.
These results were also observed when starting with all cells in the atlas sharing TCRs with the NK-like cluster ( Figure 4G – K ). Here, the patient with complete response demonstrated a continuous trajectory between NK-like and tissue-resident T cells ( Figures 4H , 4K ), which was also observed in the patient with mixed response ( Figure 4H ). The resistant and untreated patients displayed no such trajectory. Altogether these data imply interconversion between tissue-resident, NK-like, and exhausted T cells, and further suggest that such interconversion may be characteristic of response to ICB—however, further experiments will be required to validate these associations given the small sample size of our single cell cohort.
Recent studies have suggested sequence similarity among antigen- specific TCR sequences( Dash et al., 2017 ; DeWitt et al., 2018 ; Glanville et al., 2017 ; Huang et al., 2020 ; Yost et al., 2019 ). Thus, we sought to characterize sequence diversity among expanded intratumoral clonotypes. For each patient, we extracted the top 50 expanded intratumoral CDR3β sequences and calculated the Grantham distance ( Chowell et al., 2019 ; Grantham, 1974 ; Pierini and Lenz, 2018 ) between all pairs of sequences ( Figures 4L – N and S7C – E ). In the Ipi/Nivo resistant and mixed response patients we observed a neighborhood of strong sequence similarity ( Figures 4L , 4M , green box) around the most expanded intratumoral clone, suggesting CDR3 sequence convergence towards a common antigen-specific response. In contrast, no such neighborhood was observed around the most expanded clone in the complete responder ( Figure 4N and Figure S7F ). These analyses may suggest a more specific, immunodominant T cell response in the patient with complete response to ICB. Altogether, our data illuminate patterns of TCR clonotype expansion across tumor regions and highlight differences in TCR molecular diversity as a function of treatment response.
Immune populations underlying patient prognosis and response to ICB and TKIs in ccRCC
Recent studies have sought to use bulk transcriptomic gene signatures ( Brauer et al., 2013 ; Choueiri et al., 2019 ; Fehrenbacher et al., 2016 ; Fong et al., 2019 ; Ha et al., 2017 ; Hakimi et al., 2019 ; Powles et al., 2015 ; Russo et al., 2014 ; Scheller et al., 2011 ; Zelenay et al., 2015 ) to predict response to immune and targeted therapies in ccRCC. While these signatures have shown some promise, a mechanistic basis for their performance is lacking.
We first visualized the expression of clinical signature genes across all clusters identified from our scRNA analysis ( Figure 5A ). We found that both the Javelin and T effector signatures were expressed across CD8 + , CD4 + , and NK populations. The angiogenesis signature was primarily expressed on TAM, cDC2, and CD45 − populations such as vascular endothelium, PAX8 + renal epithelium, and CA9 + ccRCC. The Adenosine and Myeloid Inflammation signatures were broadly expressed amongst myeloid populations. In general, bulk RNA-seq analysis of flow sorted populations recapitulated the expression patterns observed in our scRNA analysis ( Figure S8A ). However, this analysis also indicated a trend towards enrichment of the Adenosine and Myeloid Inflammation signatures in neutrophils which were not captured in our single cell analyses. An important difference between the adenosine and myeloid inflammation signature is inclusion of CXCL5 in the adenosine signature, which was not detected in our scRNA cohort likely due to its expression on eosinophils( Egesten et al., 2001 ) and platelets ( Koltsova and Ley, 2010 ).

We next investigated the performance of each clinical signature across multiple patient cohorts. Signatures were dichotomized into high and low groups based on the median ssGSEA values in each cohort. In the TCGA ccRCC cohort, we found that signatures capturing lymphoid populations were not associated with prognosis ( Figure 5B ), while myeloid populations were associated with poor prognosis ( Figure 5C ). Moreover, the myeloid inflammation signature was associated with higher recurrence ( Figure 5C ), in line with a prior study examining the relationship between intratumoral GM-CSF expression and recurrence-free survival in ccRCC( Chang et al., 2016 ). In the TKI setting, both the adenosine and myeloid inflammation signatures were associated with poor outcome in the COMPARZ pazopanib arm ( Figure 5D ), while only the myeloid inflammation and angiogenesis signatures were associated with better outcomes in the sunitinib arm ( Figure 5E ). This result, taken together with our finding that the macrophage signature from Bindea et al ( Bindea et al., 2013 ) was not associated with outcome, suggests that differences in cell types captured by gene signatures ( Figure S8B ) have important implications for patient prognosis. We also found that the scRNA-derived signature of the TAM ISG hi population was positively correlated with the angiogenesis signature across two independent cohorts ( Figure S8C ), suggesting that this TAM population may drive response to TKIs.
We next examined clinical signatures in the IMmotion 150 cohort ( McDermott et al., 2018 ). The T effector signature was associated with better outcome in the anti-PDL1 arm, as was the Javelin signature to a lesser extent ( Figure 5F ). Both myeloid-focused signatures were also associated with poor outcome. In the combination atezolizumab plus bevacizumab arm, both the Javelin and T-effector signatures were strongly associated with better outcome ( Figure 5G ). These data not only suggest that CD8 + T cell populations may underlie response to ICB and combination regimens, but further, that additional populations—i.e. CD4 + T cells and NK cells—may drive anti-tumor immunity in ccRCC.
Our single cell RNA and TCR-seq analyses, coupled with bulk RNA-seq, suggested an increase in tissue-resident T cells following ICB. Thus, we asked whether tissue-resident T cells are associated with improved patient response to ICB in additional large cohorts. First, we sought to derive highly specific signatures for populations identified via scRNA-seq ( Table S2 ). We devised a hierarchical approach for signature generation based on successive rounds of differential expression (DEG) analysis ( Figure S9A and methods), followed by experimental validation. We validated signature specificity using bulk RNA-sequencing of immune and CD45 − populations sorted flow cytometrically from an independent cohort of ccRCC patients ( Figure S9B and methods), and focused our efforts on signatures for the CD8A + tissue-resident population and TAM ISG hi populations, since the former showed enrichment in patient with a complete response to ICB in our scRNA cohort, and the latter demonstrated associations with angiogenic gene expression both in our scRNA and bulk RNA-seq analyses. Both signatures were highly specific for their respective target populations in this independent cohort ( Figure S9C – D ; AUC = 0.99 and 0.93, respectively).
We next asked if pre-therapy levels of the CD8A + tissue-resident and TAM ISG hi signatures were associated with outcome after ICB-based combination regimens or anti-VEGF therapy in ccRCC. We obtained pre-therapy bulk RNA-seq data from the IMmotion 151 cohort ( Rini et al., 2019b ), one of the largest cohorts of ccRCC patients treated with Atezolizumab (anti-PDL1) and Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF), and applied both the prior ccRCC signatures described in Figure 5 and our CD8A + tissue-resident signature to pre-therapy bulk RNA-seq data for the cohort ( Figure 6A ), controlling for clinical covariates depending on the drug class ( table S3 ). Strikingly, none of the prior signatures were associated with outcome in this cohort, but high levels of the CD8A + tissue-resident signature predicted improved PFS (P = 0.036, HR = 0.77, 95% C.I. = 0.60–0.98). In the Sunitinib arm of the cohort, we asked if the TAM ISG hi signature was associated with outcome, given its high expression of angiogenic markers. Indeed, high levels of the signature were associated with improved PFS in this cohort ( Figure 6B ).

We next asked if we could validate these associations in a second cohort of ccRCC patients treated with similar therapies. We obtained pre-therapy bulk RNA-seq data from the Javelin 101 cohort ( Motzer et al., 2019a ) of patients treated with Avelumab (anti-PDL1) + Axitinib (anti-VEGF) or Sunitinib. In the Avelumab/Axitinib arm, high pre-therapy levels of the CD8A + tissue-resident signature were associated with improved PFS ( Figure 6C , P = 0.0014, HR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.43–0.82). This association outperformed the T effector signature in this cohort ( Table S3 , T Effector HR = 0.69) and was comparable to the Javelin signature ( Table S3 , Javelin HR = 0.60), highlighting the power and biological interpretability of scRNA-derived signatures. Moreover, high pre-therapy levels of the TAM ISG hi population were predictive of improved PFS after Sunitinib therapy ( Figure 6D , P = 0.04, HR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.57–0.99). Application of these single cell signatures to the smaller cohorts evaluated in Figure 5 showed that neither signature was associated with improved survival in the TCGA, suggesting that these populations are not simply prognostic for ccRCC outcomes ( Table S3 ). Notably, application of the CD8A + tissue-resident signature to the McDermott et al cohort revealed an increase in responders (CR/PR) relative to non-responders (PD) in both the Atezo and Atezo+Bev arms of the cohort ( Table S3 ). Finally, we asked whether application of the CD8A + tissue-resident signature to post-therapy bulk RNA-seq data from the Checkmate 009 cohort was associated with improved outcome—however, high levels of the signature did not reach statistical significance in this cohort ( Figure S9E ). This result may be due to expansion of alternative T cell populations after treatment with anti-PD1 monotherapy compared to combination therapies ( Wei et al., 2017 ), though further functional experiments will be required to investigate this possibility. This result may also be due to the heterogeneous nature of the cohort; i.e. a mix of first and second-line patients and drug dosages. Taken together, our analyses demonstrate associations between CD8A + tissue-resident T cells and TAM ISG hi cells across multiple independent cohorts of patients treated with ICB-based combination regimens or anti-VEGF therapy.
DISCUSSION
Here we dissect advanced ccRCC via spatially segregated multimodal single cell genomics. Our study provides a detailed atlas of the immune populations infiltrating ccRCC, which reveals extensive within-patient diversity in immune infiltration across untreated and ICB-treated tumors. TCR repertoire analyses revealed the phenotypes of expanded intratumoral clones and confirmed that these clones can be detected in PBMC and normal kidney.
Our data add to a growing body of literature suggesting that tissue-resident T cells may promote anti-tumor immunity in solid cancers( Djenidi et al., 2015 ; Egelston et al., 2019 ; Malik et al., 2017 ; Menares et al., 2019 ; Nizard et al., 2017 ; Park et al., 2019 ; Workel et al., 2016 ). However, we also observed expansion of a TCR clonotype associated with tissue-resident cells in the patient resistant to Ipi/Nivo therapy, suggesting that the high infiltration of TAMs in this patient limits the efficacy of anti-tumor T cell responses. To investigate this idea, we performed a receptor-chemokine analysis using CellPhoneDB, focusing on significant interactions between the TAM HLA hi / int populations and the CD8A+ tissue-resident population ( Table S4 ). CellPhoneDB identified multiple suppressive interactions in the resistant patient, i.e. between TNFRSF1A (on CD8A + tissue-resident T cells) and GRN (on TAMs). Macrophage-derived GRN has previously been shown to promote T cell exclusion in pancreatic cancer( Nielsen et al., 2016 ; Quaranta et al., 2018 ). Furthermore, we identified an interaction between ADRB2 (on CD8A + tissue-resident T cells) and VEGFB on TAMs—implying polarization towards an immunosuppressive TAM phenotype, as previously described with respect to TAMs and VEGF signaling( Kzhyshkowska et al., 2014 ). In contrast, we detected TAM-CD8A + tissue-resident interactions that may be involved in the induction of pro-inflammatory responses in the complete response patient, such as the interaction between CXCR3 (on CD8A + tissue-resident T cells) and CXCL9 (on TAMs).
We further develop an analysis framework (‘TCR trajectory analysis’) to characterize differentiation pathways between T cell subsets, founded on the assumption that sharing of TCR clonotypes imply a common lineage. This analysis provided insight into interconversion between tissue-resident, NK-like, and exhausted T cells. However, we caution that trajectory analysis does not resolve the direction of transitions between populations. The observation of trajectories between tissue resident and NK-like T cells may imply a common progenitor generating two distinct phenotypic states, in line with recent work characterizing early and differential fate commitment of naïve T cells( Kok et al., 2020 ). Our data further show that the patients with a complete response and mixed response to ICB were characterized by continuous trajectories between subsets, whereas cells from the resistant patient were in a more “fixed” state.
Importantly, our data suggest that tissue-resident CD8 + T cells expand after ICB administration in ccRCC. We detected an association between pre-therapy levels of our scRNA-derived tissue-resident signature and better response to ICB-based combination regimens (i.e. Atezolizumab/Bevacizumab or Avelumab/Axitinib) across multiple cohorts. Taken together with our data showing that a signature of TAM ISG hi cells predicted better PFS after Sunitinib therapy across multiple cohorts, our study demonstrates the power of single cell genomics for the derivation of biologically informative signatures that are comparable to or outperform previous signatures.
Our data should serve as a benchmark for future functional investigation regarding the mechanistic basis for the associations between CD8A + tissue-resident T cells with ICB response. A hallmark of tissue-resident cells is their ability to respond rapidly to bacterial or viral infections ( Park et al., 2019 )—in the context of cancer immunosurveillance, this key feature may enable CD8A + tissue-resident T cells to mediate similarly swift recognition of neoantigens and the subsequent production of cytotoxic effector molecules, as demonstrated in our data ( Figure S2D ) and by others ( Park et al., 2019 ). Future studies should examine the TCR specificity of these cells in tumors. Similarly, the ontogeny and activity of TAM ISG hi cells-defined by a unique combination of pro-inflammatory interferon stimulatory genes and canonically immunosuppressive angiogenic markers—should also be investigated.
An important limitation of our study is the small number of patients in our single cell cohort. While the total number of patients analyzed here is modest, our data provide an important addition to burgeoning Human Cell Atlas( Regev et al., 2017 ) and Tumor Atlas( Rozenblatt-Rosen et al., 2020 ) efforts. Accordingly, we anticipate that our study will serve as a critical resource for the development of new immunotherapies and combination regimens in ccRCC.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS
Human Subjects
All research activities were pre-approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board (IRB) at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and individuals were required to provide written informed consent to participate in the study. Six patients with histologically proven ccRCC were included in the analysis, their diagnosis was confirmed by expert genitourinary pathologists (S.G., Y.B.C.). All invasive procedures performed and systemic therapies administered were indicated by the patients’ treating physicians, and individuals were identified according to the pharmacologic agents given prior to tumor collection. Only one individual (Nivo-exposed) was treated as part of a clinical trial ( NCT02595918 ) which is still accruing patients. Permission was obtained from the principal investigators to publish the data from this patient, who was treated with neoadjuvant nivolumab (3 mg/kg q/2w for a total of 4 doses before nephrectomy). All other treatment regimens were administered according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines on kidney cancer( Motzer et al., 2019b ).
Patient 1 (hereafter referred to as “Untreated 1”) was a 69 year-old female with a left kidney tumor who underwent radical nephrectomy and was found to have stage III disease. The patient was eventually placed in a randomized adjuvant ICB vs placebo clinical trial. Patient 2 (hereafter referred to as “Untreated 2”) was a 57 year-old female who presented with stage IV disease involving the right kidney, adrenals, regional lymph nodes, lungs and bones. The patient underwent a cytoreductive nephrectomy and one dose of ipilumab/nivolumab but subsequently experienced rapid progression of disease and succumbed to disease 6 weeks following surgery.
Four patients in our study underwent single cell profiling after being treated with ICB or combination regimens. Patient 3 (hereafter referred to as “Nivo-exposed”) was a 54 year-old male enrolled in a clinical trial of neoadjuvant nivolumab therapy for high risk non-metastatic disease. The patient received 4 doses of Nivolumab followed by radical nephrectomy and has remained disease free for 12 months after surgery. Patient 4 (hereafter referred to as “Ipi/Nivo resistant”) was a patient with stage IV and massive retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy. The patient received ipilimumab/nivolumab for 9 months with unchanged disease despite treatment and underwent a cytoreductive nephrectomy with retroperitoneal lymph node dissection. Patient 5 (hereafter referred to as “Ipi/Nivo mixed response”) was a 64 year-old male who presented with a left-sided kidney tumor and concomitant lung metastases. The patient had a near complete response in the lung, along with shrinkage of primary tumor. A cytoreductive nephrectomy was then performed, at which point tissue was collected for single cell profiling. The patient remained with only stable lung disease, and therapy was stopped about 6 months after surgery. Patient 6 (hereafter referred to as “Ipi/Nivo complete response”) was a 63 year-old male who presented with a right-sided kidney tumor and metastatic disease to the bone and lung. The patient was placed on ipilimumab and nivolumab combination therapy, and shortly thereafter, complete regression of all metastatic disease sites was noted. However, a non-enhancing renal mass remained, and therefore a radical nephrectomy was performed. Final histologic analysis showed complete pathologic response in the primary renal mass.
Clinical information and pathologic assessment
After IRB approval, the medical records of patients who consented to this study were reviewed. Baseline demographics as well as other clinical characteristics and patient outcomes were manually reviewed by medical personnel (R.G.D.). An experienced genitourinary pathologist (S.G.) blinded to treatment status and patient outcomes performed histopathologic evaluation of the tumor, as well as the adjacent normal kidney samples. The following features were evaluated: presence of histologic tumor regression, morphologic classification of the viable tumor and localization as well as semiquantitative assessment of intratumoral immune infiltrates.
METHOD DETAILS
Sample collection, transport and initial processing
Samples were directly obtained from the operating room during nephrectomy. At the time of specimen extraction, samples of around 1–1.5 cm were obtained by the treating surgeon (A. A. H.) from spatially distinct tumor regions (at least 1 cm apart) and each one labelled according to its spatial location (relative to the adjacent kidney). For untreated patients (n = 2), 3 tumor regions and an adjacent normal kidney sample (at least 2 cm away from the tumor) were obtained. For all ICB-treated patients (n = 4), three tumor regions, an adjacent normal kidney sample (at least 2 cm away from the tumor), and PBMC sample (in cellular preparation tubes, CPTs) were obtained. In one case (Ipi/Nivo resistant patient), a lymph node sample from the adjunctive retroperitoneal lymph node dissection was also collected. In total, 29 samples with paired scRNA-sequencing and TCR-sequencing were analyzed in the study. Tissue samples were placed in separate containers containing Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium and transported in regular ice to the laboratory. The CPTs were transported at room temperature. Overall transit time in all cases was less than 1 hour (from specimen extraction to cell dissociation) and the tissue samples were kept in RPMI medium at all times. Once in the processing lab, small tissue aliquots (of around 3 mm3/each) were separated from each sample for bulk tissue profiling. One of the aliquots was snap frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 degrees C for posterior bulk sequencing profiling, while a second aliquot was placed in 10% formalin. The remainder of the tissue was kept fresh in RPMI medium and dissociated into a cell suspension.
Tissue dissociation and single cell suspensions
Kidney tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgery. Matching blood was collected in CPT tubes before surgery. PBMC from these RCC patient’s blood was obtained using the CPT tubes processing protocol (BD Biosciences). Tubes were spun (1500g × 20 minutes) and the PBMC layer was collected and washed twice (400g × 10 minutes) in incomplete cold RPMI media. Multiregional RCC tumor samples were obtained from diverse anatomical sites in chilled PBS. Each tumor sample was surgically cut and minced in small pieces in cold RPMI media. Small pieces of tumor samples along with collection volume was spun (400g × 10 minutes) to obtain a pellet. The pellet was firmly dislodged with tapping and incubated with tumor dissociation cocktail: Liberase (Roche): 250 ug/ml, DNAase (Roche): 100 Units/ml, in HBSS) at 37°C for 25–45 minutes, until good digestion was visually apparent. Afterward, cells were collected and filtered (100u). Filtered cells in enzymatic suspension were diluted with cold incomplete RPMI media and spun (400g × 10 minutes). This procedure was repeated twice to completely remove the tumor dissociation cocktail. Afterwards, the single cell suspension was frozen in CTL-Cryo ™ ABC media kit until live cell sorting with flow cytometry.
FACS cell sorting
Frozen single cells were quickly thawed in prewarmed complete media (IMDM with 10% FBS) and washed twice in complete media. Cell pellet was gently broken with tapping and cells were stained with DAPI (Thermo Scientific, Product#62248, 1:1000 dilution) and or Zombie aqua (Live/Dead ™ aqua dead cell stain kit. REF L34966 , 1:1000 dilution) to exclude dead cell during cell sorting. Samples were collected in sterile IMDM supplemented with 10% FBS. All samples were processed as soon as possible after sorting. Sorted cells were washed and suspended in cold PBS (0.4% BSA in PBS) at optimal density before loading at 10X genomics controller.
Paraffin embedding and hematoxylin/eosin (H&E) staining
The samples previously placed in 10% formalin were left in this solution at room temperature for 24 hours. They were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and placed in 70% ethanol at +4C for dehydration. After 24 hours, paraffin-embedding was performed and tissue blocks procured. Formaldehyde-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks were sliced and 5 um-thick slides obtained for staining. Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining was used for pathologic review.
Preparation of single cell RNA-seq libraries
The suspension of 10,000 FACS sorted live cells in 1X PBS (calcium and magnesium free) containing 0.04% BSA (A2153, SIGMA) was used as input to the 10X chromium controller system (10x Genomics Inc., product code 120223). The cells were barcoded using the 10x ™ GemCode ™ Technology to separately index each cell’s transcriptome by partitioning them into Gel Bead-in-EMulsions (GEMs). GEMs were generated by combining barcoded Single Cell 5’ Gel Beads, RT Master Mix with cells, and Partitioning Oil on a microfluidic chip. The GEM RT reaction was performed in thermocycler (53°C for 45 minutes, 85°C for 5 minutes, 4°C hold overnight). Post RT incubation, GEMs were broken, and first strand cDNA was recovered using DynaBeadsR MyOne ™ Silane beads. cDNA amplification was performed with PCR to generate sufficient material to construct both T cell-enriched libraries (TCR) and 5’ gene expression libraries. SPRIselect reagent ( B23318 , Beckman Coulter) was used to perform cleanup post cDNA amplification. 2ng of amplified cDNA was used for T cell target enrichment with TCR specific primers. 2ng – 50ng of amplified cDNA and target enriched product respectively was used as input for library construction. Fragmentation, end repair and A tailing was performed as per manual instruction to obtain final libraries containing the P5 and P7 priming sites used in IlluminaR sequencing. High sensitivity DNA chips and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) was used for 5’ gene expression and T cell library profile quality control and quantification. Quality control was performed twice before sequencing. 5’ gene expression library was sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 S1 with sequencing depth of approximately 300–500 million reads per sample. T cell enriched library was sequenced on NextSeq with sequencing depth of approximately 50–60 million reads per sample.
Processing of single cell RNA-seq libraries, batch effect correction, and clustering
Pre-processing of scRNA-seq fastq files was conducted using Cell Ranger v3.0.2 (10x genomics). scRNA-seq reads were aligned to the hg19 reference genome (ref-version 3.0.0), and the count matrix of cell barcodes by genes used for downstream analysis was generated using the Cell Ranger count function with parameter –expect-cells=3000. The raw count matrix for each of the 29 samples was obtained from the Cell Ranger count filter_matrix output. Briefly, for each sample, Cell Ranger plots the total UMI count against the cell barcode rank in decreasing order of total counts and filters cell barcodes out of the resulting count matrix based on the inflection point of the plot. This step minimizes the number of empty droplets that are included in downstream analyses. We created an initial count matrix combining all 29 samples from all patients. Following count matrix generation, cells with > 20% of transcripts derived from mitochondrial genes were considered apoptotic and were thus excluded. Following this step, all mitochondrial genes were filtered out of the count matrix. Ribosomal genes and the noncoding RNAs NEAT1 and MALAT1 were excluded due to prior reports of strong influence on downstream clustering( Freytag et al., 2018 ). Genes with mean raw count < 3.0 were removed from the analysis, resulting in a count matrix of 167283 cells and 16323 genes for downstream analysis. We used Seurat v2.3.4 to perform standard library size and log-normalization. The mean library size was 3586 transcripts per cell.
In our study, samples from each patient were sequenced on different days subject to dates of surgery, thus potentially introducing batch effects upon sample pooling for downstream analysis. We adopted a conservative approach to batch effect correction through the detection of mutual nearest neighbors in expression space as described in Haghverdi et al ( Haghverdi et al., 2018 ), treating each batch as the set of samples belonging to a single patient as a batch. Log-normalized counts for each of the 29 samples were used as input to the fastMNN() function from the R scran package( Lun et al., 2016 ) in Bioconductor. Samples were input in the order in which they were sequenced, and fastMNN() was used with default parameters (i.e. nearest neighbors k = 20). Briefly, fastMNN() performs an initial PCA across all cells to reduce dimensionality, followed by MNN identification and correction vector calculation. The expression profile for each cell is then batch corrected by subtracting the corresponding correction vector from its coordinates in PC space. The resulting batch-corrected PCA matrix was then input into Seurat using the SetDimReduction() function. The top 10 principal components were used as input for Louvain clustering using the FindClusters() function at resolution 0.9( Levine et al., 2015 ; Xu and Su, 2015 ). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used to visualize single cell transcriptional profiles and clusters, using the RunUMAP() function in Seurat with default parameters (i.e. n_neighbors = 30L, min_dist = 0.3). To confirm that the MNN approach successfully mitigated batch effects in the data, we computed the prevalence of each cluster (n = 31) out of all cells in each sample, followed by a centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation for compositional data( Egozcue et al., 2003 ) and PCA. This analysis demonstrated that samples clustered by tissue, not batch (i.e. patient) ( Figure S2A ).
Differential expression analyses and cell cluster annotation
All differential expression analyses were conducted using the FindMarkers() function in Seurat, with test.use = “neg binom”. Batches were specified as a six-level factor (i.e. one covariate for each patient) in the latent.vars argument. To phenotype clusters, we took a hierarchical approach to differential expression analysis coupled with manual review of marker genes. We first plotted known lineage markers for immune populations, including CD45 (pan-immune), CD3D (pan-T cell), NK1.1 (pan-innate lymphocyte), CD79A (pan-B cell) and CD14 (pan-myeloid). Within each of these broad classes, we then performed a differential expression analysis comparing each cluster within its class to all other clusters. Upon manual review of significantly differentially expressed genes from this analysis (i.e. log fold change > 0 and FDR-corrected P <= 0.05), we defined 9 major lineages- CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, NK cells, B cells, monocytes, tumor associated macrophages (TAMs), dendritic cells (DCs), mast cells, and CD45 − cells. We then performed a more focused differential expression analysis within each of these lineages, by comparing each cluster within a lineage to every other cluster within the lineage. Genes were considered marker genes for a cluster if their log fold change was > 0 and FDR-corrected <= 0.01. These genes were also considered signature genes for each cluster, and used for bulk RNA-seq analysis as described in Figure 3D – E . Given the prior reported complexity of TAM populations( Chevrier et al., 2017 ) in ccRCC, we plotted the expression of curated markers from three additional studies( Cassetta et al., 2019 ; Chevrier et al., 2017 ; Franklin et al., 2014 ) on our clusters.
Visualization of marker genes on single cell RNA-seq clusters
Marker genes were visualized on UMAP plots or on violin plots using log-normalized counts. For heatmaps of marker genes, we plotted the mean log-normalized count of each gene across all cells in each cluster displayed on the heatmap.
Correlation of cluster prevalences across scRNA-seq samples
For analyses in Figure 2H and Figure S4 , cluster prevalences were calculated as the prevalence of each immune cluster (i.e. all T, NK, B, and myeloid clusters) out of all immune clusters (n = 23). As described above, since the data are compositional, cluster prevalences were CLR-transformed prior to correlation analysis. We performed a Spearman correlation between all pairs of clusters, and corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.
Derivation and experimental validation of single cell signatures
As described in Figure S9A , we sought to derive signatures for single cell populations (in particular, the CD8A + tissue-resident population and TAM ISG hi populations) for application to bulk RNA-seq data and/or data from clinical cohorts. To enable the discovery of highly specific single cell signatures, we performed multiple rounds of differential expression (DEG) analysis as described in Figure S9A . First, we performed a “subpopulation” DEG analysis, whereby each cluster (e.g. CD8A+ tissue resident cells) was compared to all other cells in its broad lineage (e.g. all other CD8A+ T cell clusters). This round of DEG establishes the DEGs specific to the cluster (i.e. subpopulation). For each cluster, we then retained only those genes (log FC > 0; FDR P < 0.01) exclusive to that cluster’s subpopulation DEG. Subsequently, for each cluster we performed a “lineage” DEG analysis, in which each cluster (i.e. CD8A + tissue-resident T cells) was compared to all other clusters of other lineages (e.g. all CD4 + clusters, and all myeloid clusters). For each subpopulation (e.g. CD8A + tissue-resident, CD8A + exhausted, etc) within a lineage (e.g. CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, TAMs, monocytes, DCs, NK cells, B, Mast), we retained the same set of lineage genes, i.e. the set of genes that are commonly upregulated across all subpopulations of the same lineage when performing DEG analyses vs other lineages. As with the subpopulation DEG analysis, for each lineage we only retained genes that are exclusive to that lineage when compared to all other lineage signatures. To define the final signature genes for each cluster, we combined its subpopulation and lineage DEGs—and, to ensure that genes in the each signature were only expressed on immune cells and not on CD45 − cells, we filtered out genes that were expressed with FPKM > 25 in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia ( https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle ), following a similar step as in Liu et al Nature 2020 ( Liu et al., 2020 ).
We next sought to validate the specificity of these signatures in an independent dataset of ccRCC patients receiving bulk RNA sequencing. The populations used for sorting and validation are described in subsequent sections in the methods. Signatures were applied to bulk data using ssGSEA, and for each signature of interest (CD8A + tissue-resident and TAM ISG hi ) we computed the area under the ROC curve for the flow-sorted population of interest (e.g. CD8A + tissue-resident T cells) vs all other populations.
Gene signature scoring across single cell RNA-seq samples
For analyses in Figure 2I , Figure 3C , and Figure S6 , we wished to project clinical signature genes onto our scRNA samples. For each signature, we first computed z-transformed log-normalized counts for each of its genes for each cell in the dataset. Then, for each gene, we summed all z-scores across all genes in the signature to generate a signature score, which was visualized on UMAP plots as in Figure 2D , or compared across scRNA samples as in Figure 3C and Figure S6 .
Processing of single cell TCR-seq libraries
Raw BCL files were analyzed using Cell Ranger (version 3.0.2) pipeline and reference genome from 10x Genomics site ( https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-vdj/software/downloads/latest ). The “mkfastq” option was used to generate FASTQ files and the “vdj” option was used to generate single cell TCR sequences, annotations and VLOUPE visualization files. Raw read sequences were aligned to the 10X reference genome build based on Ensembl GRCh38 (refdata-cellranger-vdj-GRCh38-alts-ensembl-2.0.0). Raw data from each sample from the “filtered_contig_annotations.csv” output was further filtered by only including TCR alpha (TRA) or TCR beta (TRB) calls that was reported as “productive”.
Single cell TCR-seq clonotype analysis
TCR clonotypes were defined as the combination of Vα, Vβ, Jα, Jb, CDR3α, and CDR3β segments, together with the CDR3α and CDR3β amino acid sequences. Thus, we obtained 43828 TCR clonotypes across all samples and tissues. For the Ipi/Nivo complete responder patient, scTCR-seq data were only available from the far region. For analyses in Figure 4A – F , we chose the top 50 intratumoral TCR clonotypes ordered by occurrence in each patient tumor, and counts (i.e. occurrences) were log2-transformed. Similarly, in Figure S7A , we chose the top 50 TCR clonotypes across all tissues sampled in each patient. For analyses in Figure 4L – N , we used the top 50 CDR3β clones, since the diversity of CDR3β exceeds that of CDR3α( Woodsworth et al., 2013 ). We restricted our analyses to CDR3β sequences of the most common length in each tumor. Pairwise similarity between CDR3β sequences was calculated using the Grantham distance, a well-characterized distance metric often used for comparison of protein sequences( Grantham, 1974 ). The Grantham distance considers the physiochemical properties of amino acids and thus the functional similarity between sequences and has been used to compare the physiochemical properties of HLA alleles( Chowell et al., 2019 ; Pierini and Lenz, 2018 ). Sequence logos were generated using the ggseqlogo() package in R.
TCR trajectory analysis
We used TCR trajectory analysis to resolve potential differentiation pathways between T cell subsets. We first select a starting cluster (e.g. CD8+ tissue-resident), and pull down all cells in the atlas sharing exact TCR clonotype sequences (paired αβ sequences) with the starting cluster. TCRs in the starting cluster are limited to those that are present in at least 5 cells. We then run Palantir ( Setty et al., 2019 ) with default parameters on this set of cells, setting the starting point for trajectory analysis as a random cell in the starting cluster. Pseudotime ordering is calculated from the starting point as described previously ( Setty et al., 2019 ), and cells were visualized on a force directed layout. To mitigate batch effects, we extracted principal component coordinates for all input cells from the PCA output by fastMNN() used for batch effect correction of all cells in the atlas.
RNA extraction, quality control, mRNA enrichment and sequencing
For the ccRCC bulk RNA-seq cohort presented in Figure 3 , snap-frozen tissue was kept a −80°C until RNA extraction. Samples were thawed in regular ice and, in order to homogenize the tissue and remove the excess lipid content characteristic of ccRCC, a pretreatment process using trizol, chlorophorm and cold centrifugation was implemented. Total RNA was then extracted using the RNeasy mini extraction kit (QIAgen #74104), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For bulk RNA-seq samples used for the validation of scRNA-seq signatures as described in Figure 6 and Figure S9 , 11 additional treatment-naïve stage III ccRCC patients were analyzed, with multiple samples taken per patient for sorting of various immune and CD45- populations as described in full in Figure S9 . For bulk RNA-sequencing of these populations, populations were sorted as outlined in Figure S9B into Trizol LS and flash frozen for subsequent SMART-Seq for ultra-low input RNA-sequencing. RNA library preparation, quality control and RNA-sequencing were performed by the Integrated Genomics Operation of SKI.
Processing of bulk RNA-sequencing libraries
Bulk whole transcriptome RNA-sequencing libraries were prepared with TruSeq Stranded Total RNA library preparation kit (Illumina). Briefly, 100 ng total RNA was used as input for Ribo-Zero rRNA removal, followed by cDNA synthesis and ligation of TruSeq RNA UD Indexes. Libraries were PCR amplified and quantified with Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific), then sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 S2 or S4 flow cell with a sequencing depth of approximately 200 – 500 million reads per sample. RNA-seq raw read sequences were aligned against human genome assembly hg19 by STAR 2-pass alignment( Dobin et al., 2012 ). QC metrics, for example general sequencing statistics, gene feature and body coverage, were then calculated based on the alignment result through RSeQC. RNA-seq gene level count values were computed by using the R package GenomicAlignments( Lawrence et al., 2013 ) over aligned reads with UCSC KnownGene( Kent et al., 2002 ) in hg19 as the base gene model. The Union counting mode was used and only mapped paired reads after alignment quality filtering were considered. Finally, gene level FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase Million) and raw read count values were computed by the R package DESeq2( Love et al., 2014 ).
Gene signature scoring across bulk RNA-sequencing samples
For analyses in Figure 3D – E , Figure S8A , and Figure 5 , we applied scRNA or clinical signatures to bulk RNA-seq samples using single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)( Barbie et al., 2009 ; Subramanian et al., 2005 ). ssGSEA scores were calculated using the GSVA package in Bioconductor( Hänzelmann et al., 2013 ).
Chemokine-receptor analysis
Chemokine-receptor analysis was conducted using CellPhoneDB with default parameters as described in Efremova et al ( Efremova et al., 2020 ). Full outputs are provided in table S4 .
Clinical and survival analyses
We evaluated 5 clinical signatures in our analysis- Javelin, T-effector, Angiogenesis, Adenosine, and Myeloid Inflammation( Brauer et al., 2013 ; Choueiri et al., 2019 ; Fehrenbacher et al., 2016 ; Fong et al., 2019 ; Ha et al., 2017 ; Powles et al., 2015 ; Russo et al., 2014 ; Scheller et al., 2011 ). Clinical and survival analyses were conducted across several patient cohorts receiving bulk RNA-sequencing with various treatments and endpoints- the TCGA ccRCC cohort (both cancer-specific survival and recurrence), the COMPARZ , IMmotion 150, IMmotion 151, and Checkmate (BMS) 009. Clinical signatures were applied to bulk RNA-sequencing (TCGA, IMmotion150, IMmotion 151, Javelin 101 and Checkmate 009) and microarray (COMPARZ) data using ssGSEA, as described above. For all survival analyses, ssGSEA scores were stratified at the median, hazard ratios were calculated using a Cox regression, and P-values were calculated using the log-rank test. In the IMmotion 151 cohort, both PFS and clinical covariates (e.g. MSK risk score and sarcomatoid status) were available—thus, all signatures were adjusted for MSK risk score (high, intermediate, low) in the Atezo/Bev arm and MSK risk score and sarcomatoid status (yes/no) in the Sunitinib arm ( Table S3A ). Clinical covariates (e.g. MSK risk and sarcomatoid status) were not available in the Javelin 101 cohort. For comparisons of ssGSEA signature scores between responders (CR/PR) and non-responders (PD) in IMmotion150, a two-sided Wilcoxon test was used. For analyses in Fig S9E , bulk RNA-seq from patients after ICB administration was used for signature comparisons. The data related to application of single cell signatures to cohorts presented in Figure 5 is listed in Supplementary Table 3 —thus, both single cell signatures (CD8A+ tissue resident and TAM ISG hi) and known signatures (Javelin, T effector, Angiogenesis, Adenosine, Myeloid Inflammation) have been applied to TCGA ( Creighton et al., 2013 ) ( Figure 5 for known signatures, Table S3C for scRNA signatures), COMPARZ ( Motzer et al., 2013 ) ( Figure 5 for known signatures, Table S3C for scRNA signatures), IMmotion150 ( McDermott et al., 2018 ) ( Figure 5 for known, Table S3C for scRNA), IMmotion 151 ( Rini et al., 2019b ) ( Figure 6 for known and scRNA), Javelin 101 ( Motzer et al., 2019a ) ( Figure 6 for scRNA, Table S3B for known).
QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
All single cell differential expression analyses were conducted using the negative binomial test, controlling for batch effects (i.e. per patient). Correlations between cluster prevalences were calculated using Spearman correlation. Single cell signatures were applied to bulk RNA-seq data using ssGSEA. Signatures were dichotomized at the median value in each cohort for survival analysis. For all survival analyses, p-values were calculated using the log-rank test and hazard ratios calculated using Cox regression.
