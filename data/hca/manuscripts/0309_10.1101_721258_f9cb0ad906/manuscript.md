## Abstract

Celiac disease (CeD) is an autoimmune disorder in which ingestion of dietary gluten triggers an immune reaction in the small intestine1,2. The CeD lesion is characterized by crypt hyperplasia, villous atrophy and chronic inflammation with accumulation of leukocytes both in the lamina propria (LP) and in the epithelium3, which eventually leads to destruction of the intestinal epithelium1 and subsequent digestive complications and higher risk of non-hodgkin lymphoma4. A lifetime gluten-free diet is currently the only available treatment5. Gluten-specific LP CD4 T cells and cytotoxic intraepithelial CD8+ T cells are thought to be central in disease pathology1,6-8, however, CeD is a complex immune-mediated disorder and to date the findings are mostly based on analysis of heterogeneous cell populations and on animal models. Here, we comprehensively explore the cellular heterogeneity of CD45+ immune cells in human small intestine using index-sorting single-cell RNA-sequencing9,10. We find that myeloid and mast cell transcriptomes are reshaped in CeD. We observe extensive changes in the proportion and transcriptomes of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and define a CD3zeta expressing NK-T-like cell population present in the control LP and epithelial layers that is absent and replaced in CeD. Our findings show that the immune landscape is dramatically changed in active CeD which provide new insights and considerably extend the current knowledge of CeD immunopathology.

To assess the changes in the immune cell landscape of the small intestine in CeD, we isolated CD45+ immune cells from LP and the epithelial layer of duodenal biopsies obtained from newly diagnosed CeD patients and from individuals with normal histology (controls) ([Fig. 1a](721258v1_fig1.tif) and Supplementary Table 1). In line with previous studies[11](https://doi.org/10.1002/eji.1830230231),[12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cgh.2018.08.043), analysis of the markers used for index sorting shows that CeD harbors increased numbers of CD45+ cells in LP and a higher fraction of CD3+ intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) as compared to controls ([Extended Data Fig. 1a, b](721258v1_figE1.tif)). A modified SORT-seq[10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cels.2016.09.002) single-cell RNA-seq protocol and application of quality control cut offs yielded ∼4,000 single cells with high quality RNA profiles ([Fig. 1a](721258v1_fig1.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 1c-e](721258v1_figE1.tif)). t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) analysis revealed five major cell compartments that, based on typical lineage markers (assessed by protein and/or mRNA), were annotated as T-cells (CD3), plasma cells (PCs) (CD19 and SDC1 (CD138)), myeloid cells (CD14, CD11c and HLA-DR), mast cells (MC) (KIT) and B cells (MS4A1 (CD20)) ([Fig. 1b-d](721258v1_fig1.tif)). Cell size and granularity are in line with the classical phenotype of the cells ([Fig. 1e](721258v1_fig1.tif)). The tSNE clusters were formed by contribution from all donors with no considerable donor batch effect ([Extended Data Fig. 1f](721258v1_figE1.tif)).

We applied the Wilcoxon rank sum test (‘Seurat’ pipeline[13](https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt.4096)) to identify cell type-specific transcripts distinguishing each cell population ([Fig 1f](721258v1_fig1.tif) and Supplementary Table 2). We identified 181 genes differentially expressed in the T-cell compartment with IL7R, GZMA, RORA amongst the top genes. For PCs, 104 genes largely consisting of immunoglobulin related genes such as IGLC2, IGLC3, and IGHA1. We found 245 marker genes distinguishing myeloid cells with SELENOP, SLC40A1, HLA-DRA and CD74 among the top genes. MC analysis yielded 229 genes with the top ranked genes being HPGD, LAPTM4A, VIM and LMO4. We could identify in total 98 genes as B cell markers with CR2, RALGPS2 and FCRL2 as top ranked genes ([Fig. 1f](721258v1_fig1.tif)). CeD and control cells populate the same major cell compartments but a different pattern within the MC, myeloid and T cell compartments was observed. Virtually all CeD and control IELs clustered with the T-cell compartment ([Fig. 1g](721258v1_fig1.tif)).

tSNE analysis of the MC compartment revealed 4 clusters (C0-C3). C1 is common to controls and CeD, whereas C2 and C3 consist mostly of control and CeD cells, respectively ([Figs. 2a, b](721258v1_fig2.tif)). MCs from each donor contributed to these three clusters except for C0, which is donor-specific and was excluded from the subsequent analysis ([Extended Data Fig. 2a](721258v1_figE2.tif)). Cluster C3 cells, enriched in CeD, were smaller and less granular ([Fig. 2c](721258v1_fig2.tif)). The three clusters differ in their expression levels of C20orf194 and FKBP11 that are high in the common cluster C1, whereas REG1A and HLA-DRB1 are high in the control cell cluster C3 and low in the CeD cluster C3 ([Fig. 2d](721258v1_fig2.tif) and Supplementary Table 3).

tSNE analysis of the myeloid compartment revealed seven clusters (C0-C6) ([Fig. 2e](721258v1_fig2.tif)). Clusters C0, C2, and C4 express higher levels of CD163 and lower levels of CD11c ([Fig 2e, g](721258v1_fig2.tif)). C2 and C4 differentially express typical macrophage genes[14](https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.20170057) ([Fig. 2h](721258v1_fig2.tif) and Supplementary Table 3) reminiscent of CD11c-mature macrophages (Mf) which are noticeably underrepresented in CeD[15](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-3083.2011.02549.x) ([Fig. 2f](721258v1_fig2.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 2b, c](721258v1_figE2.tif)). Clusters C1 and C3 express higher levels of ITGAX (CD11c) and FCER1A reminiscent of immature Mf and of dendritic cells (DC)[16](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41385-018-0060-1) ([Fig. 2e, g](721258v1_fig2.tif)). CD11c-high cells were ordered from C1 to C3 and C6 cells with C3 cells differentially expressing SLC38A1, PLAC8, SERPINB9, PPA1 and FCER1A and C6 cells expressing CLEC10A, a marker associated with cDC2[17](https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2018.00744) ([Fig. 2h](721258v1_fig2.tif)). Pseudotime analysis revealed two trajectories originating from cluster C5 and diverging into a Mf and a DC path that correspond to CD11c-low and CD11c-high cells, respectively ([Fig. 2i](721258v1_fig2.tif)). C6 cells expressing CLEC10A are located between the two trajectories suggesting that this cluster may originate from both the Mf and the DC pathway[16](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41385-018-0060-1). C5 cells differentially express HELLPAR, FBXO22 and PRRG3 ([Fig. 2h](721258v1_fig2.tif)). Note that the fluorescent antibody panel used in the index sorting cannot distinguish C5 cells from the other myeloid cells ([Extended Data Fig. 2d, e](721258v1_figE2.tif)). Using linear modeling and Bayesian statistics, we identified MYBL1 as a highly expressed transcription factor (TF) in the shared origin of Mf and DC (C5) with ZEB1, IRF8 and YBX1 as the potential driver TFs of the MF path and MAF of the DC path ([Fig. 2i](721258v1_fig2.tif) and Supplementary Table 4).

Focusing on the humeral branch of the adaptive immune system, tSNE analysis identified a small cluster of B cells (C2) and two prominent clusters (C0 and C1) of PCs ([Fig. 3a](721258v1_fig3.tif)). FACS analysis showed that CD19+ PCs are significantly increased in CeD ([Extended Data Fig. 3a](721258v1_figE3.tif)). Based on transcriptome analysis, we noticed that B cells and PCs are rather equally distributed across the clusters in CeD and control samples ([Fig. 3b](721258v1_fig3.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 3b, c](721258v1_figE3.tif)). Beside subtle differences in immunoglobulin genes, no major difference between the two PC clusters could be detected in mRNA, CD19 protein expression and cell size and granularity ([Extended Data Fig. 3d-f](721258v1_figE3.tif) and Supplementary Table 5). Pseudotime analysis revealed B cells at the beginning of the path and the two PCs clusters largely intermingled along the trajectory ([Fig. 3c](721258v1_fig3.tif)). Using our linear modeling approach, we identified 1597 and 1210 genes that are gradually up- or down-regulated (Supplementary Table 6). The TFs ETS1, BCL11A, REL, and IRF8 are found to be highly expressed in B cells and their expression is downregulated in PCs. MEIS2 and RUNX1 appear to be transiently upregulated (bin 3) along the pseudotime path whereas XBP1, CREB3L2, SUB1and PRDM1 are among the highest expressed TFs in fully differentiated PCs ([Fig. 3d](721258v1_fig3.tif) and Supplementary Table 6).

tSNE analysis of the T-cell compartment ([Fig. 1b](721258v1_fig1.tif)) yielded three clusters of CD4+ T cells (C0, C2 and C4) and four clusters of CD8+ T cells (C1, C3, C6 and C7) ([Fig. 4a](721258v1_fig4.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 4a](721258v1_figE4.tif)). Three clusters (C5, C8 and C9), that co-cluster with the T-cell compartment when analyzing all CD45+ cells ([Fig 1](721258v1_fig1.tif)), turn out to be distinct and are CD4- /CD8-at mRNA levels and express no or very low levels of CD3 protein ([Fig. 4a-c](721258v1_fig4.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 4c](721258v1_figE4.tif)). Expression of the canonical GNLY [18](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.immuni.2018.09.009) amongst 29 other known and novel genes, identified cluster C8 as natural killer (NK) cells ([Fig. 4e](721258v1_fig4.tif) and Supplementary Table 7). The NK cells are mainly present in the LP and more abundant in CeD than controls ([Fig. 4 d, g](721258v1_fig4.tif)). Cells of cluster C9 were less present in CeD and identified as innate lymphoid cells class 3 (ILC3) based on their expression of PCDH9, KIT, ALDOC, AHR and RUNX2 genes as previously reported[19](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.immuni.2019.01.012) ([Figs. 4a, d, e](721258v1_fig4.tif) and Supplementary Table 7).

Strikingly, the CD4+ T cells in CeD are different from controls. While control cells dominate cluster C0 expressing among other genes IL7R, ADAM19, TNFSF13, more than 85% of cells in C2 are from CeD ([Fig. 4d](721258v1_fig4.tif)). C2 cells express genes including TBC1D4, MAF, BIRC3, PHTF2, TFRC, TIGIT, IL32, FOXO1, GBP5, STAT1, and IL6R; several of these are known to be expressed in both activated and regulatory T cells 20-22 ([Fig. 4e](721258v1_fig4.tif) and Supplementary Table 7). Gene ontology analysis showed that these cells are significantly enriched for signaling pathways including ‘Cytokine Signaling in Immune system’, ‘NOD-like receptor signaling pathway’ and ‘Signaling by Interleukins’ (Supplementary Table 7). Pseudotime analysis on C2 cells revealed 94 genes that are upregulated along the trajectory with GFI1 and MED1 as the highest expressed TFs in the end of the path ([Fig. 4f](721258v1_fig4.tif)). GFI1 and MED1 have been shown to regulate T cell activation and NKT cell development, respectively[23](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1109095108),[24](https://doi.org/10.1111/imm.12580). C4 cells are equally present in CeD and controls and express genes including INPP4B, ANK3, FOXP1 and AHNAK ([Figs. 4d, e](721258v1_fig4.tif)).

CD8+ T cells in CeD clusters are also very differently from controls. Clusters C1 and C3 contain 75% and 95% CeD cells, respectively ([Fig. 4d](721258v1_fig4.tif)). Importantly, the clusters contain the vast majority of both LP and intraepithelial CD8+ T cells ([Fig. 4h](721258v1_fig4.tif)). C1 cells differentially express KLRC2 and KLRD1 both NK markers known to be involved in the cytotoxic activity of CD8 IELs in CeD[25](https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.20060028),[26](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0016-5085(00)70173-9(2000)). They also express TRGC2 suggesting that gamma delta T cells are within this cluster. C3 cells express ITGAE and CD101, marker genes for resident memory T cells[27](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciimmunol.aav5581), and CCL5, HLA-DP and ENTPD1 (CD39), all expressed on activated CD8+ T cells ([Fig. 4e](721258v1_fig4.tif) and Supplementary Table 7). Interestingly, CD39 was recently shown to be a marker for tumor-specific CD8 T cells[28](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0130-2). Control CD8+ T cells mainly contribute to cluster C6 and highly express LRP12 and GPR155. Cluster C7 contains slightly more CeD cells that differentially express GZMK and KLRG1 suggesting they are recently recruited memory CD8+ T cells[29](https://doi.org/10.1038/nri3173) ([Figs. 4a, d, e](721258v1_fig4.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 4c](721258v1_figE4.tif) and Supplementary Table 7). Pseudotime analysis of clusters C1 and C3 revealed a developmental trajectory in which it appears that C1 cells progress towards C3 cells. Applying our linear modeling, we found that IKZF2 is the potential driving TF expressed in C1 cells and E2F3, RUNX2, and TCF3 among others in C3 ([Fig. 4f](721258v1_fig4.tif) and Supplementary Table 8).

Our analysis reveals that IELs in control individuals consist of two distinct populations: CD3low and CD3high cells. The CD3high IELs are mostly CD8+ T cells ([Figs. 4g](721258v1_fig4.tif)). The CD3low IELs are bigger in size and more granular as compared to the other cells in the T-cell compartment ([Extended Data Fig. 4b](721258v1_figE4.tif)) and are mainly CD4low/CD8low/ CD247 high (CD3 zeta) cells (cluster C5, [Figs. 4a, b, g](721258v1_fig4.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 4c](721258v1_figE4.tif)). We classify cluster C5 cells as ‘NK-T’ as they express classical NK as well as T cell genes such as TRDC, GZMA, FCER1G, CDH17, CD69, TYROBP and ID2 ([Fig. 4e](721258v1_fig4.tif) and Supplementary Table 7). This transcriptome profile is very similar to that described for natural IELs in mice[30](https://doi.org/10.1038/nri3007). The CD3low cells in C5 are not exclusively intraepithelial as ∼25% of the cells are located in the LP ([Extended Data Fig. 4d](721258v1_figE4.tif)). Moreover, C5 cells are remarkably underrepresented or absent in CeD ([Figs. 4d, h](721258v1_fig4.tif) and [Extended Data Fig. 4d](721258v1_figE4.tif)). Together, we find that the majority of CD8+ T cells in both LP and epithelium express an activated phenotype in CeD compared to controls, suggesting that CD8+ T cells in both compartments are actively involved in CeD pathology.

In conclusion, we find a dramatic change of the immune cell landscape in the active CeD lesion. The number of CD3+ T cells is increased and the majority of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells are transcriptionally very different from their control counterparts. At the same time mature Mf with regulatory properties are greatly reduced and NK-T IELs are virtually absent in CeD. Our atlas thus provides an important framework to increase our knowledge about this complex immune-mediated disease.
