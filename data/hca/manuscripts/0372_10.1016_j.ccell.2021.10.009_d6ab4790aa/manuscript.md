SUMMARY
Immunotherapy is a mainstay of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) management. While tumor mutational burden (TMB) correlates with response to immunotherapy, little is known about the relationship between the baseline immune response and tumor genotype. Using single-cell RNA sequencing, we profiled 361,929 cells from 35 early-stage NSCLC lesions. We identified a cellular module consisting of PDCD1 + CXCL13 + activated T cells, IgG + plasma cells, and SPP1 + macrophages, referred to as the lung cancer activation module (LCAM hi ). We confirmed LCAM hi enrichment in multiple NSCLC cohorts, and paired CITE-seq established an antibody panel to identify LCAM hi lesions. LCAM presence was found to be independent of overall immune cell content and correlated with TMB, cancer testis antigens, and TP53 mutations. High baseline LCAM scores correlated with enhanced NSCLC response to immunotherapy even in patients with above median TMB, suggesting that immune cell composition, while correlated with TMB, may be a nonredundant biomarker of response to immunotherapy.
Graphical Abstract
In brief
Leader et al. provide the largest NSCLC cohort analyzed by scRNA-seq and CITE-seq, demonstrating shared and variable elements of the treatment-naive immune response and identifying independent immune-modifying effects of tumor mutational burden and TP53 mutation, resulting in a refined model of how neoantigens, driver mutations, and immune state combine to drive immunotherapeutic response.
INTRODUCTION
Immune checkpoint blockade targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis is the first-line therapy for a majority of patients with metastatic and locally advanced non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) ( Remon et al., 2020 ). Although checkpoint blockade agents have improved overall survival (OS), most patients do not realize clinical benefit and still may experience physical and financial toxicity. Biomarkers to determine optimal treatment regimens are lacking, as our understanding of tumor-associated immune phenotypes and correlates of checkpoint blockade response remains incomplete.
While multiple studies have used single-cell assays to profile NSCLC tumor-infiltrating immune cells in comparison with patient-matched noninvolved lung (nLung) ( Lambrechts et al., 2018 ; Laughney et al., 2020 ), blood ( Zilionis et al., 2019 ), or both ( Kargl et al., 2017 ; Lavin et al., 2017 ), or characterized tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes ( Guo et al., 2018 ; Li et al., 2019 ; Thommen et al., 2018 ; Wu et al., 2020 ), we continue to lack a full understanding of how immune cell phenotypes vary across patients. In particular, it remains unclear which immune cell populations and phenotypes are associated with robust, tumor-directed T cell responses and response to immune checkpoint blockade, and how these features are connected to tumor-cell-intrinsic characteristics such as tumor mutational burden (TMB) ( Binnewies et al., 2018 ; Rizvi et al., 2015 ). A deeper analysis is further required for uncovering the cell types and states associated with immunostimulatory versus immunoregulatory presentation of tumor-associated antigens, as well as parsing the tumor-related effects on tissue-resident and migratory innate cell types. Attempts to integrate these data across the innate and adaptive arms of the immune system are of crucial importance to optimizing the rational design of immunotherapies. Furthermore, while response to immune checkpoint blockade has been associated with specific patient groups, individual driver mutations, the degree of immune infiltrate, and TMB, the complex interplay between these factors remains poorly understood.
Here, we sought to define the molecular immune states induced in the tumor microenvironment by profiling an expanded patient cohort compared with previous related studies via multi-scale single-cell analyses. We integrated the results of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of immune cells with cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq; Stoeckius et al., 2017 ), a method allowing for combined scRNA-seq and multiplexed single-cell surface protein measurement. We revealed a pattern of intertumor variability, which was validated in multiple bulk RNA datasets and correlated with TMB and tumor driver mutations across multiple tumor types. Finally, we found that this immune pattern conferred prognostic benefit to patients treated with checkpoint blockade.
RESULTS
Integrative analyses unify phenotypic mappings across substrates and datasets
To probe transcriptional states of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, we profiled cells from untreated, early-stage NSCLC patients undergoing resection ( Figures 1A and 1B ; Table S1 ). We generated three datasets integrating antibody profiling of surface markers using CITE-seq, scRNA-seq, and T cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq) ( Tables S2 – S4 ). We performed CITE-seq on tumor and nLung tissues from eight patients and scRNA-seq in an additional 27. CITE-seq panels included 15 antibodies to validate RNA-based cell-type annotation, and were expanded up to 81 antibodies for more specific investigation ( Table S2 ). To expand the annotation of T cells, we performed joint scRNA-seq/TCR-seq in three patients.

scRNA profiles were clustered using a batch-aware algorithm to combine data across patients while accounting for batch-specific background noise ( Martin et al., 2019 ; Figures 1C and S2A – S2C ; STAR Methods ). We first clustered 19 nLung and 22 tumor samples processed with 10X Chromium 3′ V2 chemistry, then used this model to classify cells from differently processed samples or different datasets ( Figures S1B – S1D and STAR Methods ). The RNA-based clustering identified 49 immune clusters in six compartments including T cells, B cells, plasma cells, mast cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), and mononuclear phagocytes (MNPs) (see STAR Methods for further discussion of cluster annotations). Overall, 361,929 single cells from 35 tumors and 29 matched nLung samples were classified into 30 annotated transcriptional states ( Figures 1C and S1E – S1G ). Clusters mapped between 590 and 23,812 cells, and all clusters included cells from multiple patients ( Figures 1E and 1F ; Table S5 ). CITE-seq data confirmed cell identities using well-established protein markers ( Figure 1D ). For example, pDCs expressed transcripts associated with this lineage ( LILRA4 , IRF8 ; Figure 1C ) and known population-defining proteins (CD123; Figure 1D ). To facilitate sharing of findings, we built an interactive website that enables exploration of the dataset ( https://scdissector.org/leader ).
To determine whether tissue sampling was a key source of variance, we examined data from three regions per tumor in eight patients ( Lambrechts et al., 2018 ), mapping cells to our clustering model. Clustering the samples by correlation of cell-type frequencies demonstrated that samples clustered by patient ( Figure S1H ) and that distances between patient-matched samples were reduced compared with those not patient-matched ( Figure S1I ). Therefore, while the total level of immune content varies regionally ( Rosenthal et al., 2019 ), intertumor differences drove phenotypic variability of the immune cells present.
To inspect whether immune changes between tumor and nLung were distinct across patients or, alternatively, globally similar, we estimated the immune diversity within tumor and nLung using the Euclidean distances between the log-transformed cluster frequencies. This analysis indicated that nLung samples were significantly more similar than tumor samples ( Figure 1F ; t = 8.3; p < 10 −10 ). Comparing distances among nLung and among tumor with the distances between nLung and tumor, meanwhile, showed that the diversity between independent (unmatched) tumor and nLung samples was larger than the diversity within tumor samples (t = 19.6; p < 10 −10 ) and nLung samples (t = 24.6; p < 10 −10 ), suggesting that most tumors harbored many conserved changes ( Figures 1E and 1F ).
We next asked whether the differences between nLung and tumor could be observed in an independent cohort. The cell-type frequencies of eight tumor-nLung pairs from Lambrechts et al. (2018) validated the distinct microenvironments we observed in our cohort ( Figure 1G ), demonstrating the observed tumor signature to be robust and reproducible, encouraging us to further study the transcriptional states within it.
The intratumoral DC compartment is characterized by expansion of CD14 + /CD163 + DC3
Resolved scRNA-seq and CITE-seq DC profiles included cDC1, cDC2, a cluster we called mature DC enriched in regulatory molecules (mregDC) and described with others in great detail elsewhere ( Maier et al., 2020 ; Zhang et al., 2019 ; Zilionis et al., 2019 ), and three clusters phenotypically consistent with cells in the recently refined “DC3” annotation ( Bourdely et al., 2020 ; Dutertre et al., 2019 ; Villani et al., 2017 ; Figures 2A and 2B ; STAR Methods ). Overall, DC3 was the most prevalent DC subtype and these increased in tumors, whereas mregDCs were the rarest ( Figures 2C and S2A – S2C ). As we had seen previously, the fraction of cDC1 was strongly reduced in tumors ( Figure 2C ; Lavin et al., 2017 ).

Since the activation profile of mregDC is crucial for inducing tumor-directed T cell responses ( Maier et al., 2020 ), we examined the mregDC distribution in tumors by multiplexed immunohistochemical consecutive staining on a single slide (MICSSS; Remark et al., 2016 ). We stained for DC-LAMP and PD-L1, as the transcripts encoding genes were highly enriched in the mregDC cluster ( Figure 2D ), which highlighted cells that accumulated in tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) in close proximity to T cells ( Figure 2E ). CD3-negative areas of TLS, which are putatively analogous to lymph node B cell zones, were frequently populated by MYH11 + follicular DCs ( Ioannidis and Laurini, 2019 ), a stromal cell type commonly found in B cell zones ( Figure 2F ).
To better understand the relationship between DC3 and other MNPs, we searched for mutually exclusive genes among CD14 monocytes, cDC2, and monocyte-derived macrophages (MoMΦ; Figure S2D and Table S5 ). Scoring MNP populations with these gene lists showed that DC3 were distinct from MoMΦ and CD14 + monocytes. Ordering cells within each of these compartments by the expression of the monocyte and cDC2 gene programs demonstrated anticorrelation among DC3 (ρ = −0.34, p < 10 −10 ) but not cDC2 (ρ = 0.021, p = 0.15; Figure 2G ), suggesting that DC3 existed on a phenotypic spectrum between monocyte-like and cDC2-like cells. While some MoMΦ genes were expressed in DC3 more highly than in cDC2 cells, DC3 were distinct from MoMΦ based on higher cDC2 gene expression and lower MoMΦ gene expression ( Figure S2E ).
To further uncover variable transcriptional programs among DC3 and cDC without relying on specific cell classifications, we examined gene correlations among variable genes in all DCs. This approach resulted in sets of coexpressed genes (gene “modules”) that correlated across cells, independent of cluster assignment ( Figures S2F and S2G ; Table S5 ). Multiple modules increased in tumors were mainly restricted to DC3 and cDC2 ( Figures S2H and S2I ). One of the most increased modules in tumors included cell-cycle genes (mod8), which were mainly expressed in DC3 cluster 52 ( Figures S2G – S2I ). Upregulation of many monocyte-like or MΦ-like modules (25, 14, 6) was consistent with a higher frequency of DC3 compared with cDC in tumors.
We also identified a DC module (mod28) enriched in tumor lesions ( Figures S2G and S2H , Bonferroni p adj < 0.01, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) that included CD1A and CD207 . These genes mark the cells of Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH; Allen et al., 2018 ); we therefore called this module “LCH-like.” Interestingly, IL22RA2 , encoding the interleukin-22 (IL-22) decoy receptor IL-22-BP, similarly correlated ( Table S5 ). IL-22 modulates epithelial cell growth and tissue inflammation and promotes tumor growth through induction of tissue repair ( Dudakov et al., 2015 ), while expression of the IL-22 receptor negatively correlated with survival in KRAS -mutated lung cancers ( Khosravi et al., 2018 ). These genes were induced in the cDC2 and DC3 clusters ( Figures 2H and S2J ), confirming IL22RA2 cDC2 expression in human as had been described in mouse ( Martin et al., 2014 ).

Gene module analysis across monocyte and MΦ clusters ( Figures S3E – S3H ) revealed three broad signatures consistent with genes elevated in both AMΦ and MoMΦ (module group I), MoMΦ or IMΦ (module group II), and monocytes (module group III; Figure S3E ). Individual modules were associated with cell-type annotations as well as cell states reflecting, for example, heat-shock genes (mod28, mod49), cell cycle (mod6), HLA class II expression (mod46), and glycolysis (mod2; Figure S3F ). Examining expression patterns of gene modules across MoMΦ led us to group MoMΦ clusters into subtypes I–IV: MoMΦ-II clusters expressed the highest levels of the tumor-enriched mod2 which included SPP1 and glycolysis genes ( GAPDH , ENO1 , LDHA , ALDOA , TPI1 ), and lower levels of C1Q and HLA class II transcripts (mod4 and mod46, Figures 3C , S3F , and S3H ). MoMΦ-III clusters were enriched in mod11 (including TREM2 and LILRB4 ) and mod14 (including APOE and GPNMB ).
Gene set scores based on mutually exclusive, differentially expressed genes among CD14 + monocytes, AMΦ, and MoMΦ ( Figure S3I and Table S5 ) showed that AMΦ and MoMΦ were distinct from CD14 + monocytes ( Figure 3D ) but that MoMΦ expressed a gradient of the CD14 + monocyte score ( Figure 3E ). Analysis of the gene expression patterns of hundreds of genes within the scores supported these trends ( Figure 3F ). MoMΦ clusters were also distinct from the IMΦ cluster, based on many transcripts and surface proteins ( Figures 3A and 3B ), although some MoMΦ shared IMΦ genes such as CSF1R , FOLR2 , and MERTK ( Figures 3A , 3F , and S3A ).
The predominant populations enriched in tumors were MoMΦ, while AMΦ were depleted and IMΦ frequencies were stable ( Figure 3G ). Monocyte frequencies were also decreased, possibly reflecting differentiation to MoMΦ or DC3. Given that individual MoMΦ subsets changed between nLung and tumor to different extents, we asked how these differences related to the underlying heterogeneity among MoMΦ, beyond signatures revealed by module analysis. Highly expressed genes encoding secreted factors demonstrated strong differences among MNPs ( Figure 3H ). MoMΦ-II expressed the highest levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF and IL6 , transcripts encoding the pleiotropic factor SPP1, multiple matrix metallopeptidases, and CCR2/5 ligands CCL-2 , −8 , and −7 , while other MoMΦ populations expressed less distinctive secretory profiles. Multiple MNPs expressed the CXCR3-ligand chemotactic factors CXCL-9 , −10 , and −11 including MoMΦ, DC3, and mregDC. mregDC, meanwhile, expressed cytokines and chemotactic factors associated with T cell engagement including IL12B , EBI3 , CCL17 , CCL22 , and CCL19 .

While scRNA-based clustering did not completely resolve CD4 + and CD8 + cells, CITE-seq allowed for directly separating these cells within otherwise similar groups. The T activated cluster could therefore be separated into CD4 + and CD8 + components (15.5% and 74.8%, respectively). On average, CD4 + T activated expressed higher CXCL13 , CD40LG , BCL6 , and IL21 ( Figure S4A ). We next asked whether these differences could be leveraged to distinguish CD4 and CD8 cells in samples lacking CITE-seq staining. Learning a discriminating signature from a training set consisting of T activated from two patients and testing this signature on the remaining patients with CITE-seq staining demonstrated that transcriptional-based classification guided by antibody signals was highly accurate (86% on test set; Figure S4B ). This classification further discriminated cells that uniquely expressed CD4 versus CD8A/B transcripts in the remaining cells (84% accuracy; Figure S4C ). Applying this classification separated CD4 T activated from CD8 T activated across the rest of the dataset ( Figure S4D ). Similar to a recent report ( Singh et al., 2020 ), quantifying both CD4 and CD8 T activated demonstrated correlation across tumors ( Figure S4E ; ρ = 0.58, p = 2.7 × 10 −4 ), so we continued to group them for further analysis. While T activated and T reg were the most increased T cell populations in tumors ( Figure 4C ), a cluster highly expressing cell-cycle genes MKI67 and STMN1 and surface proteins HLA-DR and CD38 was also increased (Tcycle; cluster 18; Figures 4A – 4C ). The Tcycle cluster was diverse with respect to RNA and protein expression ( Figures 4A and 4B ); analyzing the comprising cells by scores capturing genes differentially expressed among other clusters demonstrated that Tcycle is a mixture of multiple T cell phenotypes that share the cycling state ( Figure S4F and Table S5 ). While tumors expressed overall higher frequencies of cycling T cells ( Figures 4C and S4G ), T activated and T reg showed the highest frequencies of cycling cells among T cells ( Figure S4H ).
We next performed paired scRNA-seq/TCR-seq on tissues from three patients, showing that T activated was the most clonal population in tumors ( Figure S4I ). Dividing TCRs by their expansion in nLung, tumor, or both demonstrated differential phenotypic enrichment among these divisions ( Figures 4D , 4E , S4J , and S4K ). In nLung, the phenotypic distribution of T cells with TCRs either shared with tumor samples or unique to nLung was similar ( Figure 4Ei ). In tumors, however, we observed differences in phenotypic distributions between cells with shared versus tissue-unique TCRs ( Figure 4Eii ). No TNK-like cells in tumors had TCRs that were uniquely expanded in tumors. Furthermore, the proportions of Tcycle, T reg , and T activated among cells with TCRs uniquely expanded in tumors were all increased compared with their proportions among cells with TCRs present in both tissues, and these relationships were not observed in nLung. By controlling for the phenotypic distribution of cells with shared TCRs in the tumor, we found that the clonal enrichment in these populations was not simply due to enrichment of these phenotypes. Together, these findings suggest that the accumulation of T activated in tumors is at least in part due to local clonal expansion.
B cells and plasma cells are increased in tumors, but the B cell/plasma cell ratio is conserved
B cells and plasma cells were the most increased lineage among immune cells in tumors ( Figures 1C – 1E ; median of 6.4-fold change [interquartile range (IQR) 2.5–8.4] and median of 4.1-fold change [IQR 2.2–9.4], respectively). B cells and plasma cells were distinct on both the RNA and surface marker level ( Figures 4F and 4G ). Plasma cells included rare IgD + cells (cluster 43), which were also notably CD38 int . B cell frequencies overwhelmed plasma cells, with IgD + and IgM + plasma cells being the rarest, but lineage-normalized frequencies were similar between nLung and tumor ( Figures 1E and S4L ). B cells and plasma cells were therefore found to increase in tumors without significant overall perturbation of the B cell/plasma cell ratio or plasma cell isotype ratios.
Tumors exhibit a cellular module indicative of adaptive immune activation
To identify links between cell phenotypes that may drive patient diversity, we correlated lineage-normalized cell-type frequencies across tumors ( Figure 5A ), revealing high correlation among T activated , IgG + plasma cells, and MoMΦ-II; we therefore called this group collectively the lung cancer activation module (LCAM hi ). The cell types most anticorrelated with LCAM hi were B cells, T CM/naive-like-II , AMΦ, cDC1, cDC2, and AZU1 + MΦ (LCAM lo ). Sorting patients by scores equal to the geometric average of these cell types revealed that patients were well stratified in this regard ( Figures 5B – 5D ). We referred to the extrema of this stratification as LCAM hi and LCAM lo patients, respectively. Including samples from external datasets supported the overall pattern ( Figures 5B , 5D , and S5A ). This stratification was not strongly associated with changes in lineage frequencies, and both LCAM hi and LCAM lo groups displayed lineage frequency shifts in line with overall tumor versus nLung differences, including fewer NK and more B cells and plasma cells ( Figure 5C ).

To test whether LCAM hi and LCAM lo modules were robust to the clustering approach, we reclustered using Seurat ( Stuart et al., 2019 ). Certain Seurat clusters were enriched for each annotation ( Figure S5B ), and frequencies of clusters enriched for LCAM hi cell types correlated with corresponding original quantifications ( Figure S5C ). To find out whether LCAM stratification was reproducible in an independent cohort using markers based on our CITE-seq analysis, we developed a CyTOF panel to approximate the LCAM populations by gating ( Figure S5F ). Across tumors in six additional patients analyzed with this panel, lineage-normalized frequencies generally reproduced the expected correlations ( Figure S5G ).
We then performed multiplex iterative immunohistochemistry to measure the spatial distribution of LCAM cell types. LCAM hi samples were enriched in CD138 + plasma cells that were distributed among loose aggregates of PD-1 + T cells, or were found to be surrounding TLS characterized by dense aggregates of PD-1 + T cells and CD20 + B cells ( Figures 5E and 5F ). While TLS were also in LCAM lo patients, only LCAM hi TLS displayed the encompassing plasma cells. CD68 + /FABP4 − MΦ populated LCAM hi stroma where they were found in close proximity to adaptive immune cells compared with CD68 + /FABP4 + AMΦ aggregates present in LCAM lo patients ( Figures 5G and S5H ). Consistent with frequencies measured by scRNA, we visualized few CD1c + /CD68 − /CD20 − cDC2 in LCAM hi tumors compared with higher numbers in LCAM lo ( Figure S5I ).
We then applied CellPhoneDB ( Efremova et al., 2020 ) to screen for significant enrichment of ligand and receptor expression, using a dataset of secreted ligands and their experimentally validated receptors ( Ramilowski et al., 2015 ; Table S5 ). We found many cell types, including those in LCAM cellular modules, significantly enriched for such expression ( Table S6 ). To identify tumor-specific modes of intercellular signaling dysregulation that may contribute to shaping of the LCAM cellular organization, we performed a further analysis comparing differences in ligand-receptor (LR) intensity scores ( Martin et al., 2019 ) between LCAM groups, as well as between each group and respective nLung tissues ( Table S6 ). Overall, both LCAM groups demonstrated correlated LR activation in tumors compared with nLung ( Figure 5H ). In particular, they exhibited strong intensity scores between T cell CXCL13 and B cell CXCR5 ( Figure 5I ). T cells in LCAM hi tumors also produced other factors in tumors capable of stimulating B cells ( IFNG-IFNGR1 , BTLA-TNFRSF14 ; Figure 5I ). In addition, B cells from LCAM hi tumors highly expressed TNFSF9 (41BBL), which ligates TNFRSF9 (41BB), highly expressed in T activated ( Figures 4A and S5J ).
We also observed increased IFNG-IFNGR intensity between T cells and myeloid cells in LCAM hi patients ( Figures 5J and S5K ). Consistent with IFNG response ( Chow et al., 2019 ), LCAM hi patients had higher activation of the CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 axis between myeloid and T cells ( Figures 5K – 5M ). Whereas MΦ and DC3 demonstrated many conserved ligands upregulated in tumors such as IL-10 and OSM ( Figures 5K and 5L ; note highlighted LR pairs along the diagonal), tumor cDC shared few ligands between the LCAM hi and LCAM lo groups, upregulating CCL19 in LCAM hi patients and CCL17 in LCAM lo patients ( Figure 5M ); DC3 shared the latter pattern, expressing CCL17 in LCAM lo patients ( Figure 5L ). Overall, differences in LR intensities between LCAM hi and LCAM lo patients supported LCAM patient stratification, and provide insight into immune cell crosstalk possibly underlying the development of the LCAM axis.
Projection of bulk transcriptomic data onto scRNA-derived signatures reveals the LCAM hi module in independent datasets
To identify tumor-related correlates of the LCAM module in a larger cohort, we scored bulk transcriptomic signatures along an axis defined by the LCAM cell types using genes that were differentially expressed between LCAM hi and LCAM lo tumor samples and specific to the cell types comprising LCAM modules ( STAR Methods ). We found as expected that scores generated with either gene set were correlated with published estimates of total immune content in 512 lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) ( Yoshihara et al., 2013 ; Figures S6A and S6B ), whereas an ensemble LCAM score computed by the difference of these scores (LCAM hi score minus LCAM lo score) was not ( Figure S6C and Table S6 ). When controlling for the immune content, we observed negative correlations of LCAM hi and LCAM lo scores except for tumors with the lowest immune content ( Figures S6D and S6E ), suggesting that the ensemble LCAM score measures a mode of immune activation that is independent of overall immune content. We excluded samples with the lowest 20% immune content from further analysis because probing the immune signatures was likely less informative in these samples. Sorting patients by ensemble LCAM score revealed the presence of LCAM hi and LCAM lo patient groups ( Figure 6A ). Identical analysis of 110 patients in the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) LUAD cohort ( Gillette et al., 2020 ) produced a similar result ( Figure S6F and Table S6 ).

LCAM immune response correlates with tumor genotype and expression of tumor antigens
To find out whether the ensemble LCAM score is associated with specific stromal populations, we derived gene lists that were specific for individual populations identified in a public dataset of eight NSCLC patients ( Lambrechts et al., 2018 ; Table S5 ) and used these lists to quantify enrichment in TCGA LUAD data. The ensemble LCAM score correlated with a cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) score, anticorrelated with a normal fibroblast score, and strongly correlated with the difference of these scores ( Figures 6B , S6G , and S6H ). Meanwhile, it exhibited weak or absent correlations with lymphatic and blood endothelial scores ( Figure S6I ). These data suggest a link between the LCAM cellular modules and CAF, which should be explored in further detail as CAFs regulate tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte function ( Salmon et al., 2012 , 2019 ).
While the ensemble LCAM score demonstrated an increase in large tumors (t = 2.60, p = 9.7 × 10 −3 between TNM T stage = T1 and T stage > T1), we observed variable LCAM presence in tumors of all stages ( Figure S6J ). We also observed a weak correlation between the ensemble LCAM score and CD274 expression (r = 0.14, p = 2.5 × 10 −3 ). TMB, one of the most robust predictors of checkpoint response ( Samstein et al., 2019 ), was meanwhile correlated with the ensemble LCAM score ( Figure 6C ; r = 0.49 p < 10 −10 in TCGA LUAD; Figure S6K ; r = 0.44 p = 1.7 × 10 −6 in CPTAC LUAD). This correlation was greater than associations between TMB and scores measuring immune content (Immune ESTIMATE; Yoshihara et al., 2013 ; Figure S6L ) or immune state (T cell-inflamed score; Ayers et al., 2017 ; Cristescu et al., 2018 ; Figure S6M ). Correlation of individual genes with TMB was observed broadly across the LCAM hi and LCAM lo signatures ( Figure S6N ). The ensemble LCAM scores correlated with TMB to similar extents among patients of each TNM T stage ( Figure S6O ).
The ensemble LCAM score also correlated with smoking pack-years (ρ = 0.29, p = 1.9 × 10 −6 ). We therefore stratified tumors by detection of the smoking-related mutational signature of C > A transversions in specific contexts ( Alexandrov et al., 2016 ; Huang et al., 2017 ). Both tumors with and without such detection exhibited correlations between TMB and LCAM (r = 0.36, p = 3.3 × 10 −4 among patients without detection; Figure 6C ), suggesting that this relationship was independent of smoking-driven immunomodulation.
To identify features that may influence the ensemble LCAM score beyond TMB, we regressed the ensemble LCAM score onto the LogTMB and correlated candidate variables with the residuals, which quantify the difference between the observed and expected LCAM scores based on this relationship. Scores quantifying total predicted neoantigens did not correlate with these residuals ( Thorsson et al., 2018 ; Figures S6P and S6Q ), indicating that these scores did not provide more information regarding LCAM than TMB alone. However, a score quantifying total cancer testis antigen (CTA) expression did ( Thorsson et al., 2018 ; Figure S6R ; r = 0.18, p = 2.4 × 10 −4 ), suggesting that additional tumor antigens beyond mutated neoantigens may contribute to inducing the LCAM response. Overall, projection of bulk signatures onto axes defined by variation in our scRNA-seq cohort suggested that expression of the LCAM cellular module is a marker of adaptive response against mutated and ectopically expressed tumor antigens that is independent of total immune infiltration.
LCAM correlates with TP53 mutation and response to PD-L1 blockade
While LUAD tumors responsive to checkpoint blockade frequently harbor TP53 mutations, and TP53 -mutant status ( TP53 mut ) is associated with CD8 + T cells in the tumor microenvironment ( Biton et al., 2018 ; Dong et al., 2017 ), immune-related effects of individual mutations have generally not been compared with those of TMB. While TP53 mut tumors had higher ensemble LCAM scores compared with TP53 WT /( EGFR or KRAS or STK11 ) mut tumors ( Figures 7A and S7A ), TP53 mut was also associated with increased TMB ( Figures 7B and S7B ). To control for TMB, we regressed the LCAM score onto the LogTMB and asked whether any driver mutations were correlated with the residuals, revealing that TP53 mut patients had higher LCAM scores than expected ( Figure 7C ; p = 4.0 × 10 −4 ) whereas KRAS mut patients had lower LCAM scores than expected ( Figure 7D ; p = 2.7 × 10 −3 ). No similar deviation was seen in ( STK11 or EGFR ) mut patients (p = 0.38 and p = 0.52, respectively).

To identify whether these findings were present in other tumor types, we performed similar analyses on TCGA lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) cases. While LogTMB and LCAM did not correlate among LUSC cases, we found that LUSC exhibited high LCAM levels similar to LUAD tumors with similarly high TMB, suggesting less heterogeneity in antigen-driven immune response in LUSC compared with LUAD ( Figure 7E ). Similar analyses across all TCGA tumor types revealed that the LCAM score was correlated with TMB in multiple cancer types, especially breast, stomach, and bladder ( Figure 7F ). We performed a similar correlation of TP53 and KRAS status with the regression residuals as described above, finding that TP53 mut correlated with more positive LCAM scores notably in breast cancer ( Figure S7C , left). Finally, we correlated the regression residuals with CTA scores, finding positive correlation in multiple tumor types, especially breast cancer, renal clear cell carcinoma, and sarcoma ( Figure S7D ). These findings suggested that LCAM may represent a common mode of immune activation that is present in multiple cancers.
To determine whether the LCAM score correlated with immunotherapy response, we analyzed the results of the POPLAR trial in which NSCLC patients were randomized to either atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) or docetaxel chemotherapy ( Fehrenbacher et al., 2016 ). Bulk RNA and whole-exome sequencing (WES) of tumors from trial patients recapitulated the correlation between LCAM and TMB ( Figure S7E ). Cox proportional hazards regression revealed that both LCAM and TMB trended toward improved progression-free survival (PFS) in both univariate and multivariate analyses in patients receiving atezolizumab ( Figure S7F , left panels). These effects were reduced or absent in patients who were instead randomized to docetaxel treatment ( Figure S7F , right panels).
We hypothesized that continuous changes in LCAM and TMB, particularly at low values of each, may not confer strong enough differences in antitumor immune activity to generate clinical differences. Specifically, we reasoned that LCAM hi status may be particularly beneficial in patients with high TMB. We therefore introduced preselected, clinically motivated thresholds to stratify patients as LCAM hi (top 25%) or LCAM lo , and TMB hi (top 50%) and TMB lo ( STAR Methods ). This modification resulted in an increased univariate association between LCAM hi status and PFS in atezolizumab-treated patients ( Figure 7G ; median time to progression [t prog50 ] was 2.7 months in LCAM lo patients, 7.3 months in LCAM hi ; HR = 0.58, 95% confidence interval [0.35, 0.95]) which was absent in the docetaxel-treated arm ( Figure 7H ; HR = 0.95 [0.62, 1.65]). LCAM hi status demonstrated a trend toward improved OS in atezolizumab-treated patients (HR 0.62 [0.36, 1.09]) but not docetaxel-treated patients (HR = 0.99 [0.60, 1.64], Figure S7G ). Though lacking statistical significance, these effects were preserved in multivariate analyses with TMB ( Figures 7I and S7H ). Specifically, LCAM status stratified PFS among TMB hi patients ( Figure 7J , t prog50 = 2.7 months in LCAM lo patients, 11.0 months in LCAM hi ; p = 0.044). Together, these findings suggest that LCAM, while correlated with TMB, may be a nonredundant biomarker of response specifically to immune checkpoint blockade.
DISCUSSION
The present analysis of matched tumor and nLung tissues from 35 NSCLC patients provides the largest scRNA map of early-stage lung cancer immune response to date. CITE-seq analysis furthermore serves here to unite high-dimensional models of cellular classification. By integrating public datasets and leveraging CITE-seq to design complementary antibody-based assays, we demonstrate the robustness of the reported signatures across settings. Importantly, based on strongly conserved changes observed across tumor lesions, these data support the notion that common immunotherapy paradigms could benefit large subsets of patients despite disease heterogeneity.
Among tumors, patients could, however, be stratified along the dominant LCAM axis. Based on the components of the LCAM hi and LCAM lo cellular modules, we propose that LCAM hi patients are undergoing a more vigorous antigen-specific anti-tumor adaptive immune response. Unbiased LR analyses, meanwhile, demonstrated differential LR intensities across multiple immune compartments. While the effects of individual signaling axes remain to be mechanistically interrogated in the setting of human lung cancer, the described interactions have known relevance to adaptive immune activation and provide support to the LCAM stratification that was performed on the basis of cell-type frequencies alone.
Computing an ensemble gene score learned from the LCAM hi and LCAM lo scRNA-seq tumor samples and associated cell types across broader datasets of paired bulk transcriptomics and WES identified an association with TMB and CTA score, and similar relationships were identified in multiple tumor types. This finding builds on previous work demonstrating that exhausted CD8 + T cells and MoMΦ correlated with TMB in LUAD ( Kim et al., 2020 ) by demonstrating that this correlation extends more broadly to other innate and adaptive immune states in the LCAM hi and LCAM lo cellular modules, holds across pathological stages, and is modified by other tumor-intrinsic factors. Given that TMB holds predictive power with respect to checkpoint response in NSCLC ( Rizvi et al., 2015 ; Samstein et al., 2019 ), the relationship between TMB and LCAM in treatment-naive patients suggests that this effect may be mediated by conditioning of the immune system that exists prior to treatment, and that measurement of LCAM may provide a more direct indicator of the immune system’s propensity for checkpoint response. Specifically, the evidence that many factors significantly associate with the LCAM score, not just TMB, suggests that the immune system integrates multiple signals to establish its set point. However, given limited data evaluating the independent effects of LCAM and TMB on immunotherapy outcomes, more data are needed to establish the extent to which LCAM measurement improves on the use of TMB alone.
Common LUAD driver mutations EGFR and STK11 had little effect on the LCAM response beyond that explained by their association with TMB. While STK11 mutation status is considered the most prevalent genomic driver of primary resistance to checkpoint blockade ( Biton et al., 2018 ; Skoulidis et al., 2018 ), there were no patients with STK11 -mutated tumors in our scRNA-seq cohort, so this effect cannot be addressed here. Compared with what was expected based on each tumor’s TMB, TP53 mut correlated with an intensified LCAM response and KRAS mutation, a diminished one. A similar effect between TP53 mut and inflammation was seen in breast cancer ( Wellenstein et al., 2019 ). While this inflammation led to increased metastasis in mouse models, it will be important to understand whether TP53 mut may also confer enhanced susceptibility to immunotherapy.
While the LCAM hi cellular module consists of cells undergoing an apparent active immune response, this study does not demonstrate the extent to which the module is instigated or perpetuated in situ . Specifically, despite evidence of clonally expanding T activated , it remains unclear whether these lineages are primed in situ or in tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLN). It will be important to correlate the cell types present in the tdLN to determine whether the LCAM response depends on lymph node priming, and to develop a deeper understanding of the spatial dynamics of the LCAM cell types. Furthermore, while NSCLC histological subgroups had different distributions with respect to LCAM score, an examination of epithelial cell phenotypes may provide important insights into how tumors elicit and respond to the described modes of immune response.
Overall, the present model identifies an immune activation signature, derived from immune phenotypes defined by scRNA-seq and CITE-seq, that behaves as an integrator of tumor-associated antigen load and driver mutation status, is not related to overall immune content, and correlates with response to checkpoint blockade. We believe that this axis, therefore, can serve as a more direct measure of antigen-specific, antitumor immune activation compared with previously suggested immune readouts.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS
Tissue processing
Tissues were rinsed in PBS, minced and incubated for 40 minutes at 37°C in Collagenase IV 0.25 mg/ml, Collagenase D 200 U/ml and DNAse I 0.1 mg/ml (all Sigma). Cell suspensions were then aspirated through a 18G needle ten times and strained through a 70-micron mesh prior to RBC lysis. Cell suspensions were enriched for CD45 + cells by either bead positive selection (Miltenyi) per kit instructions or FACS sorting on a BD FACSAria flow sorter (as indicated in Table S1 ) prior to processing for scRNAseq or CITEseq.
ScRNA- and TCR-seq
For each sample, 10,000 cells were loaded onto a 10X Chromium single-cell encapsulation chip according to manufacturer instructions. Kit versions for each sample are indicated in Table S1 . Libraries were prepared according to manufacturer instructions. QC of cDNA and final libraries was performed using CyberGreen qPCR library quantification assay. Sequencing was performed on Illumina sequencers to a depth of at least 80 million reads per library.
TCRseq was performed using the Chromium Single Cell 5’ VDJ kit, following manufacturer’s instructions. For patients 695 and 706, cells were subject to a CD2+ bead enrichment (Miltenyi) instead of CD45+ enrichment prior to encapsulation.
CITEseq
For each sample, cell suspensions were split and barcoded using “hashing antibodies” ( Stoeckius et al., 2018 ) staining beta-2-microglobulin and CD298 and conjugated to “hash-tag” oligonucleotides (HTOs). Hashed samples were pooled and stained with CITEseq antibodies that had been purchased either from the Biolegend TOTALseq catalog or conjugated using the Thunder-Link PLUS Oligo Conjugation kit (Expedeon). Sample hashing schemes and CITEseq panels are detailed in Tables S1 and S2 , respectively. Stained cells were then encapsulated for single-cell reverse transcription using the 10X Chromium platform and libraries were prepared as previously described ( Stoeckius et al., 2017 ) with minor modifications. Briefly, cDNA amplification was performed in the presence of 2pM of an antibody-oligo specific primer to increase yield of antibody derived tags (ADTs). The amplified cDNA was then separated by SPRI size selection into cDNA fractions containing mRNA derived cDNA (>300bp) and ADT-derived cDNAs (<180bp), which were further purified by additional rounds of SPRI selection. Independent sequencing libraries were generated from the mRNA and ADT cDNA fractions, which were quantified, pooled and sequenced together on an Illumina Nextseq to a depth of at least 80 million reads per gene expression library and 20 million reads per ADT library.
MICSSS
FFPE tissues were stained using multiplexed immunohistochemical consecutive staining on a single slide as previously described ( Remark et al., 2016 ). Briefly, slides were baked at 37°C overnight, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated in decreasing concentrations of ethanol. Tissue sections were incubated in citrate buffer (pH6 or 9) for antigen retrieval at 95°C for 30 minutes, followed by incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide and in serum-free protein block solution (Dako, X0909) before adding primary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. After signal amplification using secondary antibody conjugated to streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase and chromogenic revelation using 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole, slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, mounted with a glycerol-based mounting medium and scanned for digital imaging (Pannoramic 250 Flash III whole-slide scanner, 3DHISTECH). Then the same slides were successively bleached and re-stained as previously described ( Remark et al., 2016 ). Primary antibodies used were: anti-human CD10 (200103, R&D systems), DC-LAMP (1010E1.01, Novus biologicals), pan-cytokeratin (AE1/AE3, Dako), PD-L1 (E1L3N, Cell Signaling Tech), PD1 (EPR4877(2), Abcam), CD20 (L26, Dako), CD138 (MI15, Dako), CD68 (KP1, Dako), CD1C (OTI2F4, Abcam), and FABP4 (AF3150, R&D). Scan registration and generation of pseudo-immunofluorescent overlays were performed in QPath ( Bankhead et al., 2017 ) and Fiji ( Schindelin et al., 2012 ).
CyTOF analysis
CyTOF analysis was performed as previously described ( Martin et al., 2019 ). Briefly, cell suspensions were incubated in Rh103 intercalator (Fluidigm; 1:500) at 37°C for 20 min to label nonviable cells. Cells were washed and then stained with a panel of CyTOF antibodies for 30 minutes on ice. Cells were then fixed in 1.8% isotonic PFA with 250 nM Ir (Fluidigm) with .25% OsO4 for 30 min before stored in PBS containing 0.2% BSA at 4°C until acquisition. Immediately prior to acquisition, samples were washed with PBS and de-ionized water and resuspended to 1 million cells/mL in deionized water with a 1:20 dilution of EQ Four Element Beads (Fluidigm). The samples were acquired on a CyTOF2 (Fluidigm) equipped with a SuperSampler fluidics system (Victorian Airships) at an event rate of < 500 events/second. Gating analysis was performed using Cytobank (Mountain View, CA).
Bulk RNAseq library generation for the POPLAR trial
Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue was extracted utilized the High Pure FFPET RNA Isolation Kit. Quality and quantity were assessed by Qubit and Agilent Bioanalyzer. The Illumina TruSeq RNA Access kit was used for RNA-seq library preparation per manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, RNA was fragmented and stranded cDNA libraries were created. Libraries were then enriched for the coding transcriptome by exon capture with biotinylated oligos.

Unsupervised batch-aware clustering analysis
Immune cells from tumor and nLung samples were filtered for cell barcodes recording > 500 UMI, with < 25% mitochondrial gene expression, and with less than defined thresholds of expression for genes associated with red blood cells and epithelial cells ( Table S5 ). Cells were clustered using an unsupervised batch-aware clustering method we recently described ( Martin et al., 2019 ) with minor adjustments. This EM-like algorithm, which was also based on earlier studies ( Jaitin et al., 2014 ; Paul et al., 2015 ), iteratively updates both cluster assignments and sample-wise noise estimates until it converges, using a multinomial mixture model capturing the transcriptional profiles of the different cell-states and sample specific fractions of background noise. As opposed to other standard clustering approaches, this probabilistic method explicitly distinguishes between the measurements of transcripts from individual cells from generative models of expression built using cluster averages ( Sarkar and Stephens, 2021 ) and noise approximations. The use of such a model facilitates both the clustering of cells from distinct batches as well as the classification of cells from additional data that were not used in the initial clustering. We clustered 19 nLung and 22 tumor samples jointly and 46 additional tumor and nLung samples were mapped onto the final model as described below.

Differential expression testing
Differential expression between two sets of cells was performed using a permutation test as previously described ( Martin et al., 2019 ). Briefly, gene expression was estimated over each set as the fraction of UMIs mapping to each gene. The magnitude of differential expression was computed as the log-fold-change between the two sets for each gene. The sampling distribution under the null hypothesis (no differential expression) was then estimated by randomly shuffling the cells of the two sets 20,000 times while maintaining the sizes of the sets and calculating the log-fold-change between the permuted sets for each permutation. The empirical p value was then calculated using the rank of the absolute value of observed log-fold-change of each gene within its empirical null-hypothesis fold-change sampling distribution.

Determination of sample-sample distances
Sample-sample distances were computed as the Euclidean distance between vectors consisting of the Log 10 -transformed cell type frequencies, where frequencies were computed as a fraction of total immune cells. A regularization factor of 10 −3 was applied prior to applying the log-transform.
Determination of myeloid cell type-specific gene scores
Lists of mutually-exclusive genes were used to compare monocytes, cDC2, and MΦ in Figure 2 , and monocytes, AMΦ, and MoMΦ in Figure 3 . For these analyses, genes were identified as “mutually exclusive” if the average expression was at least 2x greater in a given population than in the other comparison populations and the average differential expression was significant with p adj < 10 −3 . To account for the large diversity of MoMΦ clusters, the maximum average expression of each MoMΦ subtype was used instead of the overall average expression. To include more AMΦ genes, a Log2FC threshold of 0.75 instead of 1 was used in the comparison between AMΦ and monocytes. Resulting gene lists are presented in Table S5 . Cells were scored according to the resulting gene lists as the Log-transformed fraction of UMI belonging to the gene list. Histograms were generated with the R function density using default parameters.
Gene-modules analyses
Gene-gene correlation modules were generated using a similar method to that previously described ( Martin et al., 2019 ). Briefly, cells were downsampled to 2000 UMI prior to selecting a set of variable genes, similar to the selection of genes in preparation for seeding the clustering. The gene-gene correlation matrix for this gene set was then computed for each sample over the cell population of interest. Correlation matrices were averaged following a Fisher Z-transformation. Applying the inverse transformation then resulted in the best-estimate correlation coefficients of gene-gene interactions across the dataset. Genes were clustered into modules using complete linkage hierarchical clustering over correlation distance. Histograms of module expression scores were generated with the R function density using default parameters. Resulting genes are listed in Table S5 .
Classification of CD4+ versus CD8+ T activated cells
CITEseq staining on a subset of patients was used to build a gene-set-based classifier that could use mRNA UMI data to discriminate CD4+ versus CD8+ cells within the T activated cluster. To identify these gene sets, cells from 2 patients used as a training set (695 and 706, chosen based on superior resolution of CD4 and CD8 CITEseq staining within the T activated cluster) were gated based on a Log 2 FC of raw ADT counts of raw CD4/CD8 > 1 and compared by differential expression. Genes were filtered by expression >10 −4 and a Log 2 FC > 1, and nonspecific or noise-related genes such as those associated with cell-cycle, long-non-coding RNAs, heat shock proteins, immunoglobulin genes, ribosomal proteins, XIST , and histone transcripts. Resulting gene lists are reported in Table S5 . Cells were scored based on the fraction of RNAs belonging to the resulting gene lists, and a discrimination threshold for the ratio of the CD4 vs. CD8 gene lists was determined based on the overall accuracy in discriminating between CITEseq-defined CD4+ vs. CD8+ cells in the training set. This gene score discriminator was validated using cells from a test set comprised of cells from 4 additional patients analyzed by CITEseq (584, 593, 596, 630), and on cells with unique detection of either CD4 or at least one of ( CD8A , CD8B ).
Analysis of cycling T cell cluster
To analyze the phenotypic makeup of the cluster of T cells expressing cell-cycle genes, we generated gene sets based on the other T cell phenotypes described here to score each cell within the cluster. To do this, we pooled the cells of each other T cell phenotype to compute its average expression. We then identified a gene list for each phenotype defined by expression > 1 × 10 −5 and Log2FC > 0.25 compared to the maximum of the other phenotypes. From this list, we excluded variable TCR genes, and other genes associated with noise or cell stress. The gene lists for the T naive/CM-like cell types were grouped, since these phenotypes were very similar. Resulting gene lists are reported in Table S5 .
For each cell in the cycling cluster, we then computed the fraction of UMI belonging to each gene signature after removing UMIs belonging to the list of genes associated with the cycling cluster that was calculated as above. We performed spherical k-means clustering using the function skmeans () in the skmeans R package on these signature fractions in order to group cells within the cycling cluster according to phenotypic subtype by spherical k-means cluster.
Single-cell TCRseq analysis
Single T cells were grouped by clonotype according to their precise combination of α and β chains present (uniquely defined by CDR3 sequence and V, D, and J gene usage), with the following acceptations in order to filter for high quality singlets:
Cells with contigs encoding > 3 productive α and β chains were excluded as multiplets.
Cells with contigs encoding > 3 productive α and β chains that completely overlapped with observed cells within the multiplets were also excluded as multiplets.
Remaining cells with 3 unique α and β chains that could be uniquely associated with similar cells displaying 2 unique α and β chains were assumed to be clonally related, whereas cells that could be similarly associated with multiple distinct sets of cells expressing 2 unique α and β chains were excluded as doublets.
Cells in which a single TCR chain was observed were assumed to be clonally related to any cells with 2 unique α and β chains to which they uniquely associated.
Remaining cells in which a single TCR chain was observed were excluded if they matched ambiguously to multiple cells with 2- or 3-chains.
Clonality scores were computed for each T cell type in each patient as 1-Peilou’s evenness over the set of unique TCRs as previously described ( Kirsch et al., 2015 ) after sampling 30 cells per T cell phenotype per sample.
Seurat clustering analysis
Samples used for the original clustering model ( Table S7 ) were used to perform a validation clustering analysis with Seurat v4.0 in R v.4.0.4. Cell filtering and gene selection was performed as above. 2000 cells were randomly selected from each sample for inclusion in the analysis. Count matrices were log-normalized and scaled. Batch integration was initiated using the Seurat function FindIntegrationAnchors () ( Stuart et al., 2019 ). Neighbor analysis was performed by invoking FindNeighbors () using the default k parameter of 20 and the first 15 principle components. Clustering was then performed with FindClusters () at high resolution ( resolution = 6 , resulting in 81 clusters) in order to ensure the model sufficient freedom to capture the variation represented by the clustering using our batch-aware multinomial mixture model-based pipeline.
Seurat analyses were performed using the 22 original patients used for clustering. Corresponding clusters were identified by examination of the heatmap in Figure S5B . Corresponding Seurat cluster IDs were: T activated : 60; IgG+ plasma cells: 10; MoMac-II: 25, 30; AMΦ: 13, 26; AZU1 MΦ: 24, 64; T CM/Naïve-II : 1,5; cDC1: 57; cDC2: 14, 40.
CellPhoneDB analysis
CellPhoneDB was implemented as previously described ( Efremova et al., 2020 ) with minor modifications using Python v3.7. To simplify the analysis, a custom database was constructed comprising a shortened, curated list of ligands and their experimentally validated receptors ( Ramilowski et al., 2015 ; Table S5 ) using the cellphonedb database generate function with option –user-interaction-only . Ligand-receptor interaction scores were computed and tested using cellphonedb method statistical_analysis , executed on the tumor samples of both LCAM hi and LCAM lo patients ( Table S7 ). 250 cells per patient, downsampled to 2000 UMI per cell, were used for the analysis. –threshold was set to 0.01 and other parameters were left as default.
Ligand-receptor analysis
Ligand-receptor intensity scores for a set of secreted ligands ( Ramilowski et al., 2015 ; Table S5 ) were calculated as previously reported ( Martin et al., 2019 ). Briefly, for each ligand-receptor interaction, for each source cell type and each receiver cell type, the intensity score was equal to the product of ligand generation from the source cell type relative to the total RNA with the expression of the receptor on the receiver cell type. Scores were independently calculated for LCAM hi and LCAM lo patient sets in nLung and Tumor tissues. To determine these patient sets, patients were sorted by the geometric mean of lineage-normalized cellular frequencies of LCAM hi and LCAM lo cell types, and the top half of patients were defined as LCAM hi with the bottom half defined as LCAM lo . Only patients analyzed using 10X Chromium V2 with immune cells purified with CD45+ magnetic bead selection were used for this analysis. The patients included in these groups were: LCAM hi : (408, 403, 522, 371, 570, 714, 584, 377, 406, 564, 630, 578, 514); LCAM lo : (571, 596, 393, 593, 626, 378, 370, 410, 572, 558, 581, 596, 729).
Identification of LCAM hi and LCAM lo bulk-RNA gene signatures
To define genes that could probe the presence of LCAM hi or LCAM lo cell types in bulk RNA data, we adopted a similar strategy to that used previously for the projection of bulk data onto signatures defined by cellular axes as measured with scRNA ( Martin et al., 2019 ; Neftel et al., 2019 ). All cells from LCAM hi and LCAM lo patients were pooled, and differentially expressed genes ( FDR < 10 −3 and Log2FC > 1) were retained. Genes that were expressed in the filtered epithelial cells > 2x higher than in immune cells on average were removed, in addition to genes excluded during the preliminary clustering analysis ( Table S5 ), all heat shock protein genes, surfactant genes, secretoglobin family genes, genes encoding B cell receptor junctional segments, and histone genes. Among the remaining differentially expressed genes, those that were expressed on average within any LCAM hi or LCAM lo subtype with Log2FC > 3 compared to the highest expressing subtype in the opposite group were retained. These gene lists were further abbreviated to include no more than 10 genes per cell type, in order to balance the number of genes coming from any individual cell type. In order to increase the differential expression effect sizes observed, only the most extreme 6 LCAM hi and LCAM lo patients processed with CD45+ bead enrichment and 10X Chromium V2 were included in the differential expression analysis. These patients were LCAM hi : (408, 403, 714, 522, 371, 570), and LCAM lo : (571, 596, 393, 593, 626, 378). For LCAM hi and LCAM lo patients, an even number of immune cells (1409) were sampled from each patient prior to pooling for the differential expression analysis.
Calculation of LCAM hi , LCAM lo , and ensemble LCAM scores in bulk-RNA datasets
Bulk RNA expression datasets were log-transformed and z-scored. For each cell type associated with LCAM hi or LCAM lo , the resulting z-scores of the associated genes were averaged. A summary average of these values was then computed across all the cell types associated with either LCAM hi or LCAM lo cell types. The ensemble LCAM score was defined as the difference (LCAM hi score) – (LCAM lo score).
Published statistics for TCGA lung adenocarcinoma patients
Estimates of total immune content present in each TCGA sample (ESTIMATE score; Yoshihara et al., 2013 ) were download from https://bioinformatics.mdanderson.org/public-software/estimate/ .
Scores associating mutational signatures ( Alexandrov et al., 2016 ) with individual TCGA samples were downloaded from the mSignatureDB website http://tardis.cgu.edu.tw/msignaturedb/ ( Huang et al., 2017 ). For the present study, detection of Signature 4 was used to indicate presence of smoking-related mutations.
Counts of Indel Neoantigens, SNV Neoantigens, and CTA score in TCGA cases were accessed from Table S1 of ref ( Thorsson et al., 2018 ).
Generation of stromal cell type scores
Fibroblast and endothelial cell count matrices from tumor and nLung of 8 NSCLC patients ( Lambrechts et al., 2018 ) were downloaded from https://gbiomed.kuleuven.be/english/research/50000622/laboratories/54213024/scRNAseq-NSCLC . Previously-applied cluster annotations were assumed, where endothelial cluster 6 was defined as “lymphatics”, and endothelial and fibroblast clusters were defined based on enrichment in tumor or nLung: endothelial clusters 3 and 4 were pooled as “Tumor BEC”, endothelial clusters 1 and 5 were pooled as “Normal BEC”, fibroblast cluster 1 was defined as “Normal fibroblast”, and fibroblast clusters 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7 were pooled as “CAF”. For each of these cell types, gene scores were defined based on a minimum average expression of 10 −4 and a minimum fold-change threshold of 4 compared to any other stromal cell type. Cell type gene-scores were defined in TCGA lung adenocarcinoma using the average z-scored gene expression of each stromal gene list.
RNAseq analysis for the POPLAR trial
RNA-seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using GSNAP ( Wu and Nacu, 2010 ; Wu et al., 2016 ) allowing a maximum of two mismatches per 75 base sequences. Transcript annotation was based on the Gencode genes database (GENCODE 27). Gene expression was quantified using the number of reads mapping unambiguously to the exons of each gene. Raw counts were adjusted for gene length using transcript-per-million (TPM) normalization, and LCAM scores were computed as described above.
WES and TMB calculation for the POPLAR trial
Whole-exome libraries were prepared from paired tumor and normal (peripheral blood mononuclear cells) DNA using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon v4.0 per manufacturer’s instructions. To analyze the resultant data, reads with low nucleotide qualities (70% of bases with quality <23) were removed and FASTQ reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using GSNAP. Duplicate reads in the resulting were marked using PicardTools, and indels realigned using the GATK IndelRealigner tool. Variants were called using MuTect ( Cibulskis et al. 2013 ) and Strelka2 ( Kim et al., 2018 ) and the intersection was utilized for downstream analyses. Tumor mutational burden per megabase was calculated by dividing the total number of nonsynonymous mutations by the coding regions captured (34.2 MB).
POPLAR outcome analysis
Progression-free survival was defined as the time of the first occurrence of RECIST v1.1-defined disease progression ( Eisenhauer et al., 2009 ), or death from any cause. Continuous association of LCAM and TMB or categorical association of LCAM hi and TMB hi status with PFS and OS were assessed using univariate and bivariate Cox Proportional Hazards modelling in R ( coxph () function). Patients with the 25% highest LCAM scores were defined to be LCAM hi based on the expected immunotherapy response rate. Patients with the 50% highest TMB were defined to be TMB hi , which aligned with an approximate threshold of 10 Mut/MB according to independent Foundation Medicine testing, matching the FDA’s tumor-agnostic immunotherapy approval threshold.
