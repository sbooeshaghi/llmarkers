Abstract
Cells in human milk are an untapped source, as potential “liquid breast biopsies”, of material for investigating lactation physiology in a non-invasive manner. We used single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to identify milk-derived mammary epithelial cells (MECs) and their transcriptional signatures in women with diet-controlled gestational diabetes (GDM) with normal lactation. Methodology is described for coordinating milk collections with single cell capture and library preparation via cryopreservation, in addition to scRNA-seq data processing and analyses of MEC transcriptional signatures. We comprehensively characterized 3,740 cells from milk samples from two mothers at two weeks postpartum. Most cells (>90%) were luminal MECs (luMECs) expressing lactalbumin alpha and casein beta and positive for keratin 8 and keratin 18 . Few cells were keratin 14 + basal MECs and a small immune cell population was present (<10%). Analysis of differential gene expression among clusters identified six potentially distinct luMEC subpopulation signatures, suggesting the potential for subtle functional differences among luMECs, and included one cluster that was positive for both progenitor markers and mature milk transcripts. No expression of pluripotency markers POU class 5 homeobox 1 ( POU5F1 , encoding OCT4) SRY-box transcription factor 2 ( SOX2 ) or nanog homeobox ( NANOG ), was observed. These observations were supported by flow cytometric analysis of MECs from mature milk samples from three women with diet-controlled GDM (2-8 mo postpartum), indicating a negligible basal/stem cell population (epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM) /integrin subunit alpha 6(CD49f) , 0.07%) and a small progenitor population (EPCAM + /CD49f + , 1.1%). We provide a computational framework for others and future studies, as well as report the first milk-derived cells to be analyzed by scRNA-seq. We discuss the clinical potential and current limitations of using milk-derived cells as material for characterizing human mammary physiology.
Keywords: Single cell RNA sequencing, human milk, milk-derived cells, human lactation
Introduction
Human milk is the optimal source of nutrients and critical bioactive factors required for human infant growth and development. However, for many women there are physiological impairments that make breastfeeding challenging [ 1 ]. This is especially true for women with obesity and metabolic disease [ 2 - 10 ], and insufficient breastfeeding is subsequently linked to long term metabolic risks for both offspring [ 11 - 13 ] and mothers [ 14 - 19 ]. Investigating underlying lactation impairments in humans is limited by the ethical and practical hurdles of directly studying mammary tissue during the period of lactation. Previous groundbreaking attempts at characterizing the human mammary epithelial cell (MEC) transcriptome have utilized cytoplasmic crescents enveloped in milk fat globules (MFGs) as they are secreted from the mammary epithelium. These studies have demonstrated stage-specific regulation of nutritional and immunological milk components as well as sustained expression of the specialized genes necessary for milk synthesis and secretion [ 20 - 22 ]. MFG transcriptomes have proven to be more representative of MECs than transcriptomes isolated from whole mammary tissue, where adipocyte and stromal transcripts are abundant [ 23 , 24 ]. However, studies that may be performed using MFG cytoplasmic material are limited by the fragility of MFGs and their limited cellular material, with only 3-8% containing small cytoplasmic crescents in the majority of milk samples [ 25 ].
Living cells present in human milk are an attractive target for the study of lactation, as “liquid breast biopsies” of milk are much more readily accessible from lactating women than tissue biopsies. These cells hold promise to represent intact MECs from lactating women, allowing for a wide array of molecular and cellular investigations to gain insight into the differentiation and functionality of the mammary gland. Such studies could identify cellular causes of lactation insufficiency and pave the way for the development of therapeutic interventions. Indeed, cells found in bovine milk have been shown to be representative of the lactating mammary epithelium [ 26 ]. However, the diversity of cell types present in milk complicates efforts to use milk-derived cells to investigate MEC-specific biology. Morphological analyses, as well as candidate studies of mRNA and protein expression have identified these cells as predominantly mature luminal MECs (luMECs) and lymphocytes [ 27 ]. The latter cell type decreases in number as milk transitions from colostrum (13-70% lymphocytes) to maturity (0-2% lymphocytes), except when elevated in instances of maternal or infant infection [ 28 - 31 ]. Other milk-derived cells are thought to include mammary progenitors and pluripotent stem cells, which upon in vitro culture may differentiate into luminal and basal MECs as well as cells from all three germ layers [ 32 - 37 ], although the stem cell population may be most prevalent in colostrum or milk from the involuting gland [ 34 , 31 , 38 , 39 ]. While these studies give us a powerful glimpse into milk-derived cell types and biology, a detailed investigation of human milk-derived cell types is necessary to fully understand their potential diagnostic utility.
Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been used to define transcriptional profiles of tissue-derived MEC samples and to interrogate differentiation dynamics during glandular morphogenesis, pregnancy and lactation in the murine mammary gland [ 40 - 44 ] and in the non-lactating human mammary gland [ 45 - 47 ]. Here, we use this technology to conduct in-depth analyses of human milk-derived cell populations during early lactation from women with diet-controlled (not requiring medication) gestational diabetes (GDM) who had normal lactation. We provide our computational strategy and analysis code to support similar endeavors by other researchers in the field. Additionally, we detail efforts to increase the practicality of studying milk-derived cells and corroborate our results with cell surface marker-based flow cytometric analyses of mature milk samples (2-8 months postpartum). Unbiased cluster analysis revealed previously undescribed functional variability among milk making cells, with some MEC populations expressing high levels of transcripts indicative of a mature milk secretory signature. We did not detect any expression of genes indicative of pluripotent stem cells or myoepithelial or basal cells. Altogether, our results demonstrate the feasibility of a workflow whereby capturing and characterizing live luMECs can be conducted to utilize human milk-derived cells as a proxy for the lactating mammary gland. Our findings provide a highly detailed view of human lactation and form the basis for new research questions. We provide methods and processing code to equip other researchers to explore human milk-derived MECs and address their potential physiological relevance.
Materials and Methods
Participants and milk sample collection.
Milk-derived cells were collected as an ancillary study of 59 mothers enrolled in a randomized controlled trial of diet composition among women with mild (diet-controlled) gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM; Clinical Trials # NCT02244814 ) [ 48 , 49 ]. Samples from five of these women are described here. The five mothers who provided these samples were exclusively breastfeeding at the indicated timepoints. They all delivered term infants and were between the ages of 28-38, with BMIs between 29.3-37.9 at delivery. Mature milk samples from three of the five women were collected at either their 2-, 4.5-, or 8-month (complementary foods were also being given at this timepoint) postpartum study visits for flow cytometric analysis of MEC cell surface markers and evaluation of cryopreservation, described below. Samples from the other two women were collected at two weeks postpartum for scRNA-seq and matched for BMI (33.1 and 33.7 at their 2-week postpartum study visits) and parity (both multiparous), given that postpartum stage, BMI and parity can affect milk production [ 50 - 52 ] and potentially milk-derived cellular composition [ 29 - 31 ]. Neither of these two women reported delayed secretory activation or low milk supply via the Infant Feeding Practices 2 questionnaire [ 53 ].
Milk collection protocols were approved by the Colorado Multiple Institution Review Board (COMIRB #14-1538) and all participants gave their informed consent. Briefly, mothers fasted overnight and while still fasting, collected a small aliquot of milk (<1oz) at home in the morning, prior to a morning or early afternoon study visit. Samples were not standardized with regard to the time of feeding or pumping (i.e. before, during, after). Milk samples were placed in a cooler on ice and transported to the study visit, at which point they were immediately transported to the laboratory from the clinic.
Cell cryopreservation.
Human milk samples arrived in the laboratory on ice and were centrifuged to pellet cells (all centrifugation steps were for 10 mins at 500xg). The resulting pellets were washed once with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and either stained immediately and/or cryopreserved for downstream analysis by resuspending in FBS+10% DMSO (ThermoFisher) prior to slow-freeze cryopreservation in a Styrofoam container.
Flow cytometry.
For scRNA-seq, cells were thawed quickly in a 37°C water bath, washed 1X with PBS to remove cryoprotectant and then stained for viability with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (FVD; ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. FVD is a fixable, intact membrane impermeant dye that stains free amines, resulting in higher fluorescence in dead cells with permeable membranes and requires staining in protein free PBS. Cells were filtered through a 35μm cell strainer (ThermoFisher) and FVD-low, viable cells were isolated by fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) on a BD FACSAria prior to analysis by scRNA-seq. For flow cytometric analyses, the FVD staining step was combined with surface marker staining using fluorescently conjugated antibodies (all ThermoFisher) against platelet and endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (CD31, eFluor 450), protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type C (CD45, eFluor 450), CD49f (PE-Cy7) and EPCAM (eFluor 660) prior to acquisition on a BD LSRII. An average of 120,198 cells ± 222,657 (SD) were analyzed to evaluate the effects of cryopreservation. Gates were set using samples stained with a single antibody and fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls. Analyses between fresh and cryopreserved cells (paired t-tests) were calculated using GraphPad Prism software. Summary statistics are listed as means ±SD.
Single cell RNA sequencing.
Viable cells, as determined above, were adjusted to 700 cells/uL and used as input to the 10x Genomics GEM capture and 3-prime V3 single cell sequencing library preparation according to the manufacturer’s protocols by the University of Colorado Genomics Core. FASTQ files for each sample were processed using Cell Ranger 3.1.0 (10x Genomics) with GRCh38 as the reference genome, and the resulting individual data were then aggregated and normalized using Seurat v3.0.2 in an R v3.5.1 environment. Data were filtered, normalized, scaled and analyzed using the R package Seurat v3.0.2 (SatijaLab). Briefly, the raw cellranger sparse matrix was imported into Seurat, cells expressing fewer than 250 unique gene counts were removed, and the sparse matrix was converted into a Seurat object. Cells with >6,000 unique genes, more than 40,000 unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, and/or mitochondrial transcript percentages higher than 40 were removed in order to exclude potential doublet or empty cell captures, resulting in 3,740 analyzable cells. Normalization, integration, and scaling were performed using the corresponding Seurat functions prior to principle component analysis (PCA). The use of integration allows common cell types to cluster together despite originating from different individuals. Based on the PCA elbow plot, 9 principal components were selected to perform Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Following UMAP, a resolution of 0.35 was selected to obtain 8 clusters using a shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization-based clustering algorithm. To determine if contributions from expression of cell cycle related genes might have influenced overall cluster patterns, the aforementioned process was performed again with the additional step of regressing out cell cycle genes. Such regression had little effect on the resulting UMAP and was not used in further analyses. SingleR v1.0.0 [ 54 ] was then used to quickly identify particular cell populations. This package compares data to a pre-annotated reference sample; in this case the reference sample used was from the Human Primary Cell Atlas [ 55 ]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cluster, or cluster markers, were found by comparing the expression of each gene within a cluster to all remaining cells. To speed up computation time and reduce the number of tests performed, only genes with expression in >50% of the cells in either comparison group, and a ln(fold change) >0.22 or <−0.22, were tested. A cluster marker was considered unique if it was identified in a single cluster, although this does not preclude expression of that gene by cells in other clusters. UMAP coordinates were exported for use in the scRNA-seq browser, Loupe (10x Genomics). Expression values (UMI counts) are not normalized when viewed in Loupe but are useful for exploration of cell signatures by biologists. See the Sup. Methods (markdown) file for detailed descriptions and complete R scripts and Sup. Tab. 1 - 4 for other analytical resources.
Pseudotime analysis
Cell data were formatted for use with Monocle3 v0.2.1 from Seurat within R. A new UMAP was generated using the default protocol shown on the Monocle3 website ( https://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle3/docs/introduction/ ), using 9 principal components again as the input. Pseudotime was calculated using all partitions to facilitate the identification of progenitor cells. The resulting pseudotime was stored in the Seurat object to view in the original UMAP space. Pearson correlation was used to identify genes with a linear relationship to pseudotime.
Pathway analysis.
Genes correlated with pseudotime, based on Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r>0.20), were found using cells populating all clusters except 3 and 6, and uploaded and analyzed for biological inference and contributions of canonical pathways using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA, Qiagen). The two output values for each unique pathway (ratio and −log(p-value)) were plotted as a scatterplot using GraphPad Prism to visually identify the canonical pathways most strongly correlated with pseudotime. Pathway analysis (IPA) was also conducted using statistically significant cluster markers with q-values of <0.05 and fold changes >10% (ln(1.11)). Pathways associated across clusters ordered by median pseudotime were sorted by significance (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR) and colored by Z-score, when available (white = N/A).
Results
Identification of milk-derived cell populations and assessment of cryopreservation
Efforts to utilize milk-derived cells for the study of mammary biology are complicated by variability among mothers, potential dynamic diversity of milk-derived cell populations present at different stages of lactation, and logistical concerns regarding asynchronous milk collection between participants. In an effort to align the logistics of clinical milk collection with single cell capture and library preparation, we evaluated whether a commonly used cryopreservation method allow for later recovery of viable milk-derived cells prior to scRNA-seq.
Freshly collected, mature milk samples (2, 4.5, and 8 months postpartum) from three women ( Fig. 1a ) were centrifuged to isolate the cellular fraction from the more buoyant MFGs. A previous report found that the cellular fraction of human milk contained both live and dead cells [ 56 ]. Accordingly, we stained and gated milk-derived cells for viability to exclude dead and dying cells in order to minimize sample variability arising from differing percentages of dead cells ( Fig. 1b , Sup. Fig. 1 , Fig. 2a ). We combined cell viability staining with immunostaining for cell surface markers of mammary epithelial cells established in non-pregnant and non-lactating human tissue (CD49f and EPCAM) [ 57 , 58 ], and cells of endothelial and hematopoietic lineage (CD31 and CD45) to identify epithelial and non-epithelial cell populations in human milk. We did not exclude erythrocytes by glycophorin A (CD235a) staining, as is usually included in lineage analyses of the digested mammary gland, because we did not anticipate that milk samples would be contaminated with blood. Consistent with previous reports showing that mature milk samples from healthy mothers with healthy infants have only a small percentage of immune cells [ 28 - 31 ], we found that combined, live endothelial and immune cells constituted only 1.4% ±1.1% of the total number of cells in the mature human milk samples we collected ( Fig. 2a & e ). After gating out endothelial and immune cells, we evaluated expression of CD49f and EPCAM in the remaining cells to identify MEC populations ( Fig. 2b & f - i ). Most of the remaining cells were CD49f − /EPCAM − (89.9% ±3.7%; Fig. 2b & g ) and relatively few cells (8.9% ±3.8%; Fig. 2b & h ) had the CD49f − /EPCAM + signature characteristic of mature luMECs from non-lactating women [ 57 , 58 ]. We also found that only a small percentage of cells (1.1% ±0.3%; Fig. 2b & i ) were CD49f + /EPCAM + , indicative of a luminal progenitor population [ 57 , 58 ], and a negligible number of cells (0.07% ±0.06%, Fig. 2b ) had the CD49f + /EPCAM − signature of basal/myoepithelial cells [ 57 , 58 ]. The relatively low number of EPCAM + cells in human milk is striking, because in non-lactating human mammary tissue, CD49f − /EPCAM − cells are considered to be stromal cells [ 57 , 58 ], which are not thought to be present in substantial numbers in milk. Thus, limited numbers of EPCAM + cells in human milk samples potentially reflects downregulation of epithelial cell adhesion molecule ( EPCAM ) gene expression in terminally differentiated luMECs of lactating women in comparison to what has been shown in non-lactating human mammary tissue [ 57 , 58 ]. Alternatively, it could reflect loss of the EPCAM antigen from MECs upon their transfer into milk.

Utilization of fresh samples necessitates the coordination of sample collection and sample processing. This approach limits the ability to process multiple samples side-by-side to minimize batch effects and restricts possibilities to match sample populations for various characteristics, such as time after delivery and maternal BMI, both factors that can dramatically influence lactation phenotypes under consideration. To increase logistical flexibility for the use of milk-derived cells in research, we tested the effects of cryopreservation on aliquots of the same samples described above using the CD49f/EPCAM staining strategy. While predictably, there was lower viability overall after cryopreservation of the CD31 − /CD45 − (Lin − ) fraction (72.3% ±14% fresh vs 37.3% ±10.0% cryopreserved, p=0.004; Fig. 2a , c & f ) there were no statistically significant effects on the populations observed in the live mammary fractions as proportions of the Lin − gate, except for a small increase in the relative viability of CD49f + /EPCAM + luminal progenitors (1.1% ±0.3% fresh vs 2.0 ±0.2%, p=0.03; Fig. 2a - d & g - i ). Interestingly, there was a trend toward increased relative viability of the live CD31/CD45 + combined endothelial/immune fraction after cryopreservation (1.4% ±1.1% fresh vs 4.3% ±2.3% cryopreserved, p=0.08; Fig. 2a , c & e ). Having established the feasibility of using cryopreserved milk-derived cells, we next characterized their individual gene expression patterns by scRNA-seq.
Single cell RNA sequencing identifies functional diversity among milk-derived cells
We performed scRNA-seq on milk-derived cells from two exclusively breastfeeding, parity- and BMI-matched (33 & 34 kg/m 2 ) women at two weeks postpartum to characterize milk-derived cells in greater detail. Cryopreservation allowed for batched processing during the scRNA-seq cell capture and library preparation steps of the 10x genomics workflow ( https://www.10xgenomics.com ; Fig. 1c ). Cells were quickly thawed after cryopreservation and sorted by FACS to collect live cells only, without additional staining for cell surface markers. A total of 4,807 individually barcoded cells were detected following the scRNA sequencing of milk-derived cells from the two human milk samples (referred to here as A and B). Aggregation of the individual sequencing captures yielded 398,425,131 total sequencing reads with an average of 82,884 reads per cell, sample A had 99,669 and sample B had 69,897 reads per cell. The fraction of mapped transcriptome reads with cell-associated barcodes was 88.7%, the median number of genes per cell was 951, and median UMI counts per cell was 4,588. Data were processed and filtered according to the methods, leaving 3,740 individual cells within the aggregated data set (1,992 and 2,612 from participants A and B, respectively), and cells from each participant populated all the clusters ( Sup. Methods , Sup. Tab. 5 ). Unsupervised clustering identified 8 clusters ( Fig. 3a , Sup. Tab. 5 ). The top 10 upregulated, non-redundant genes in each cluster are shown as a heatmap ( Fig. 3k ) and the top 12 cluster markers, including those that are redundant, are listed in Tab. 1 and displayed as UMAP overlays ( Sup. Methods , Sup. Figs. 2 - 9 ). A full list of cluster markers is provided in Sup. Tab. 6 - 13 and genes unique to each cluster are noted. We did not observe an appreciable cell cycle signature contributing to the clustering, but found evidence that cells populating cluster 7 may be progressing through the cell cycle ( Sup. Fig. 10 ).

As milk-derived cells have been reported to express both luminal and basal cell markers [ 32 , 33 ], we examined expression of keratins characteristic of luminal ( KRT8 & KRT18 ) and basal ( KRT14 ) MECs ( Fig. 3b - d ). The vast majority of cells were KRT8 & KRT18 expressing luMECs, found in all clusters except 3 and 6. KRT8 + / KRT18 + cells expressed high levels of lactalbumin alpha ( LALBA ) & casein beta ( CSN2 ) ( Fig. 3e & f ), consistent with alveolar epithelial cells that have undergone secretory differentiation. Very few cells (0.2%) (8/3,740) with detectable KRT14 were observed (cluster 2; Fig. 3d ), and there was little to no expression of genes indicating myoepithelial function or protein C receptor ( PROCR ) status [ 40 ] ( Sup. Fig. 11 ), suggesting that the milk samples we analyzed lacked appreciable quantities of basal cells. We expected a small fraction of milk-derived cells to be immune and/or endothelial cells based on our flow cytometric analyses ( Fig. 2 ) and previous reports [ 28 - 31 ]. Clusters 3 and 6 contained the only cells expressing protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type C ( PTPRC ), which encodes the hematopoietic and immune cell marker CD45 ( Fig. 3g ). Markers consistent with T cells and natural killer cells were enriched in cluster 3, (85% and 36%, respectively) and markers consistent with monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells were enriched in cluster 6 (35%, 43% and 16%, respectively; Sup. Fig. 12 ). A single cell expressing an endothelial cell signature was detected in cluster 0 ( Sup. Fig. 12 ), suggesting that exclusion of CD31 + cells is not necessary when purifying milk-derived MECs.
Human milk has been reported to contain mesenchymal and/or pluripotent stem cells [ 32 - 34 , 36 ] based upon in vitro culture evidence of milk-derived cells differentiating into cell types resembling adipocytes, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, hepatocytes, β cells and neurons, although not all reports consistently demonstrate differentiation. A large range (1%–96%) of cells expressing pluripotency markers OCT4, SOX2 and NANOG is thought to exist in milk samples [ 36 - 38 ] and milk-derived cells have been reported to take up residence and differentiate into neurons in nursing mouse pups [ 59 ]. In contrast, we did not detect transcripts encoding OCT4 ( POU class 5 homeobox 1, POU5F1 ), SOX2 or NANOG in our scRNA-seq dataset ( Fig. 3h - j ), a core set of transcription factors that maintain pluripotency. In addition, with regard to expression of other stem cell markers (SSEA4, encoded by fucosyltransferase 4, FUT4 ) and TRA-1-60/81, encoded by podocalyxin like ( PODXL ) previously reported in milk-derived cells [ 36 , 37 ], we either did not detect expression or did not find evidence of a separate cluster of pluripotent stem cells ( Sup. Fig. 13 ).
To assess whether mammary progenitors are present in milk, we examined expression of progenitor markers CD44 molecule (Indian blood group), CD55 molecule (Cromer blood group), aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A3, integrin subunit alpha 6, which encodes CD49f, and CD14 molecule ( CD44, CD55, ALDH1A3, ITGA6 and CD14 , respectively) [ 40 , 41 , 47 , 57 , 58 , 60 ] in our scRNA-seq dataset. We detected elevated expression of these markers in a subset of cells in cluster 4 ( Fig. 4a - e ). To identify the population of cells with the highest coordinate progenitor marker expression, we assigned a progenitor “score” based on the geometric mean of these markers and applied the score to all 3,740 cells. We then assigned the cells that had scores greater than the 2.0 cutoff to a separate subcluster representing putative mammary epithelial progenitors (Prog; Sup. Figs. 14 - 15 , Fig. 4f & g ). Cluster markers for the Prog subcluster are listed in Tab. 1 and Sup. Tab. 14 . This subcluster, which contains EPCAM hi / ITGA6 hi cells ( Sup. Fig. 16 ), is likely populated by cells similar to those previously demonstrated to differentiate in vitro [ 32 - 37 ]. However, the subject of future research will be to investigate the differentiation capacity of milk-derived cells based on this cell surface marker signature.

Interestingly, cells in the progenitor cluster also express transcripts produced by secretory MECs ( LALBA , CSN2 ; Fig. 3e & f ), suggesting that they may closely resemble the alveolar progenitors described by Bach et al. [ 40 ] in pregnant and lactating mice, which exist in a partially differentiated state. Cells in the progenitor subcluster also showed high expression of genes previously reported to indicate mammary stem-ness in milk-derived cells ( keratin 5 ( KRT5 ), prominen 1 ( PROM1 , encoding CD133), nestin ( NES ), integrin subunit beta 1 ( ITGB1 , encoding CD29), kruppel like factor 4 ( KLF4 ) [ 32 - 34 , 36 ], Sup. Fig. 17 ). The appearance of partially differentiated cells in milk suggests that the differentiation dynamics of luMECs might be on display in this cell population. We performed pseudotime analysis (Monocle 3) to order the milk-derived cells based on gene expression profiles that indicate transition from one state into another (i.e. least to most differentiated). The Prog subcluster was selected as the root (dark blue). We observed a gradual transition from the Prog cluster, through clusters 7 and 4, into cluster 2, and ending in clusters 1, 0 and 5 ( Fig. 4h ). Gene expression values in all cells except those in clusters 3 and 6 were then correlated with pseudotime progression, and genes with a moderate correlation (coefficient <−0.20) were input for canonical pathway analysis. The most prominent pathway negatively correlated with pseudotime was distinguished by ribosomal transcripts that are elevated in early pseudotime and turned off as cells mature (EIF2 signaling; Sup. Fig. 18 , Sup. Tabs. 15 & 16 ). This signature is reminiscent of the MFG transcriptome identified in transitional milk [ 22 ], where there was transiently elevated expression of ribosomal proteins, in preparation for the major protein synthesis required during mature lactation. We found enrichment of this “transitional” signature in clusters 2 & 7 ( Sup. Fig. 19a ). We then conducted pathway analysis across clusters using statistically significant markers identified within each cluster. We found elevated EIF2 signaling and oxidative phosphorylation in clusters 2 & 7, decreased mTOR and integrin signaling and sustained sirtuin signaling across pseudotime ( Fig. 4i , Sup. Tabs. 17 & 18 ). These patterns are consistent with key features of lactation, including mitochondrial function, protein synthesis and cellular morphology, possibly indicating the dynamic regulation of these pathways across MEC maturation. These results align with pathways identified using genes correlated with pseudotime ( Sup. Fig. 18 ). It remains to be seen how the specific genes populating the identified pathways are implicated with lactation.
Enrichment analysis was more challenging among transcripts that were positively correlated with pseudotime, as lactation pathways are not well annotated for comparison. We therefore generated our own gene sets describing expression of hormone receptors that drive functional differentiation of MECs and synthetic enzymes responsible for different milk components ( Sup. Tab. 19 ) [ 61 - 63 ]. Intriguingly, we found very little expression of the steroid hormone receptors estrogen receptor 1 ( ESR1 ) and progesterone receptor ( PGR ) ( Fig. 5a ). Hormone sensing cells are distinct from secretory cells, and hormone receptor expression is downregulated after hormonal stimulation in pregnancy [ 40 ]. We did find evidence of expression of other hormone receptors in the progenitor population, including the glucocorticoid receptor, nuclear receptor subfamily 3 group C member 1 ( NR3C1 ), prolactin receptor ( PRLR ), insulin receptor ( INSR ) and adiponectin receptor 1 ( ADIPOR1 ). These findings suggest that these hormones are critical for initiating a cascade of signaling events driving secretory activation but are not necessary for maintenance of the manufactured machinery. This interpretation is in line with the negative correlation of the glucocorticoid signaling pathway with pseudotime ( Sup. Tab. 16 ) and decreasing mTOR and PI3K/AKT signaling across pseudotime ( Fig. 4i , Sup. Tab. 17 & 18 ). Consistent with their place at the peak of pseudotime among MEC cell types, MECs in cluster 5 expressed the highest levels of milk protein and lipid synthesis transcripts ( Fig. 5b - d ), including fatty acid synthase ( FASN ), ATP citrate lyase ( ACLY ), acetyl-CoA carboxylase ( ACACB ), and the unique thioesterase needed for medium chain fatty acid synthesis, oleoyl-ACP hydrolase ( OLAH ). MECs in Clusters 0 and 5 expressed the highest levels of transcripts driving lactose synthesis ( Fig. 5e ), with cluster 0 maximally expressing LALBA and solute carrier family 50 member 1 ( SLC50A1 ), which encodes the sugar transporter SWEET1 and cluster 5 maximally expressing UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase 2 ( UGP2 ) and beta-1,4-galactosyltransferase 1 ( B4GALT1 ). Cholesterol synthesis transcripts were highly expressed in cluster 5 and maximally expressed in cluster 7 ( Fig. 5f ), possibly due to the generation of plasma membranes in cells progressing through the cell cycle. Collectively, these profiles largely reflect the pseudotime profile of milk-derived MECs, with clusters 5 and 0 representing fully matured secretory MECs (secMECs) with potential functional distinctions. Cells in cluster 5 exhibit expression signatures consistent with elevated milk protein genes and components of the MFG (cytoplasmic lipid droplet coat proteins and triacylglyceride core), whereas cells in cluster 0 express elevated levels of the primary carbohydrate (lactose) synthetic genes.

To further characterize the cell types present, we interrogated our milk-derived scRNA-seq data using previously defined gene expression signatures [ 22 , 20 , 46 , 40 ]. Clusters 1, 0 and 5 exhibited the highest expression of transcripts that were most highly expressed in bulk RNA-seq- [ 22 ] and microarray- [ 20 ] generated MFG transcriptomes from mature milk samples ( Sup. Fig. 19b & c ). The scRNA-seq signatures of clusters 1, 0 and 5 also corresponded with the scRNA-seq signature ( CSN2 & LALBA ) defining alveolar MECs in pregnant and lactating mouse mammary glands [ 40 ]. Additionally, cluster 5 had the highest expression of solute carrier family 34 member 2 ( SLC34A2 ), an indicator of MECs which have undergone secretory differentiation [ 64 - 66 ] ( Sup. Fig. 19b ). Importantly, we did not find substantial overlap between our dataset and the transcriptomic profile of mature luMECs from the non-lactating human mammary gland [ 46 ] ( Sup. Fig. 20 ), which highlights the considerable transcriptional changes arising from secretory differentiation and secretory activation needed for copious milk production, not reflected in the resting, non-lactating gland. Notably, we found that EPCAM expression was low across the mature luminal clusters ( Sup. Fig. 16a ). This result agrees with our flow cytometric analysis and is consistent with evidence that EPCAM is down regulated in terminally differentiated mammary epithelial cells [ 67 ]. The near absence of EPCAM transcript in human milk-derived cells appears to explain our flow cytometry results showing that the majority of cells in milk are CD49f − /EPCAM − , although possible contributions from EPCAM loss at the cell surface cannot be ruled out. Regardless of the explanation, our data provide evidence that EPCAM is not a practical surface marker for isolation of milk-derived MECs.
Single cell RNA sequencing analysis allows for identification of putative MEC subtypes within the milk-derived cell population, which are not easily determined from bulk expression analysis of heterogenous milk-derived MECs. We detected putatively novel lactation markers specific to luMEC progenitors or the secMEC population clusters 0, 1 and 5 ( Tab. 1 ). In early differentiated MECs (cluster 4, including progenitors), transcriptional regulators Kruppel-like factor 6 ( KLF6 ), immediate early response gene 2 ( IER2 ), Jun proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit ( JUN ), JunD proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit (JUND), Fos proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit ( FOS ), E74 like ETS transcription factor 3 ( ELF3 ), and activating transcription factor 3 ( ATF3 ) appear to be important regulators in maintaining ‘transitional’ mammary epithelial cells, consistent with prior studies of breast cancer. Potentially novel secMEC (clusters 0,1 and 5) factors include cystatin-C ( CST3 , cysteine proteinase inhibitor), chordin like 2 ( CHRDL2 , inhibits BMP activity), sterol carrier protein 2 ( SCP2 , mediates lipid transfer between membranes), phosphatidylethanolamine binding protein 1 ( PEBP1 , binds phosphatidylethanolamine), choline phosphotransferase 1 ( CHPT1 , formation of vesicular membranes), aconitase 1 ( ACO1 , iron sensor), and clathrin heavy chain ( CLTC , coated pits and vesicles). Among the identified cluster markers, cell surface proteins may facilitate separation of these different populations in future flow sorting applications. Transmembrane 4 L six family member 1 ( TM4SF1 ), tumor associated calcium signal transducer 2 ( TACSTD2 ), tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 12A ( TNFRSF12A ), and possibly claudin 4 ( CLDN4 ) encode cell surface markers in progenitor luMECs. Cell surface markers encoded by lymphocyte antigen 6 family member D ( LY6D ) and neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 2 ( NTRK2 ) could potentially be used to isolate the majority of luMECs during flow sorting ( Sup. Figs. 3 , 4 & 9 ).
Discussion
Viable cells in human milk hold tremendous potential for application in a wide array of ex vivo molecular and cellular investigations to gain insight into the functional development of the mammary gland. Readily accessible as “liquid breast biopsies”, these cells hold promise to identify physiological drivers of lactation insufficiency, paving the way for the development of therapeutic interventions. Here we describe methods for the collection and cryopreservation of human milk-derived MECs, as well as provide code for processing and interpreting the scRNA-seq data obtained from these samples. Our results describe the first reported transcriptome of human milk-derived cells at single cell resolution and identify important avenues for future research. To the extent that milk-derived cells are representative of those in the intact mammary gland, their transcriptomes largely resemble secretory alveolar MECs with the addition of a small fraction of immune cells. We also identified cells that appear to represent a range of secMEC maturation states, which may be extremely useful in interrogating the differentiation trajectory and functional status of the human mammary gland in future studies.
Analytical roadmap
Cryopreserved milk-derived cells were sorted for viability by flow cytometry and used as input for scRNA-seq using the 10x Genomics scRNA-seq platform. Following sequencing, our approach consisted of the following:
Cellranger (10x Genomics) was used to align data to the GRCh38 genome to create an unnormalized aggregated sparse matrix.
Seurat (SatijaLab) running in R was used to filter, normalize, scale, and integrate the data. Differentially expressed genes were calculated, and cluster marker overlays were generated. Data were plotted and visualized by heatmap in R using Seurat.
Monocle3 (Trapnell-Lab) was used to facilitate progenitor cell identification using Pearson Correlation.
Biological inference was made by exploring marker gene lists from each individual cluster, using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis for canonical pathways correlated with pseudotime, and SingleR [ 54 ] to assist in identifying the immune cell component.
Literature-derived functional categories were superimposed upon clusters to examine the coordinate regulation of lactation-specific gene groups needed for milk production.
Low abundance cell types
We did not detect expression of myoepithelial cells or other basal MECs, nor did we detect markers of pluripotency, as others have reported [ 36 - 39 ]. It is possible that there are technical explanations for these differences. Although we did not see substantial differences between cell populations present in fresh vs cryopreserved cells by flow cytometric analysis of EPCAM and CD49f, we cannot rule out the possibility that some populations were preferentially lost during cryopreservation. The DMSO used for cryopreservation has the potential to contribute to cellular loss, through effects on membrane permeability in some cells, resulting in their removal prior to scRNA-seq. Another possibility is that basal cells may not have been fully dissociated and exist as aggregates, which were removed by filtration prior to exclusion of doublets and selection for impermeable cells by FACS. We did not trypsinize the cell pellet prior to filtration which may have allowed for the capture of aggregated cells.
Alternatively, milk from mothers in this study may have contained fewer basal cells. Elevated BMI is linked to a diminished basal layer in the non-lactating state [ 68 ] and participants had an average BMI of 34.4 at delivery. Additionally, we characterized only 4,604 total cells after cryopreservation and cell sorting, which represents a relatively small milk volume. Beginning with larger volumes may be important in future studies, as cell concentration has been shown to range from 10,000-13,500,000 cells/mL in human milk samples [ 27 ]. To resolve low abundance cell types based on scRNA-seq transcriptomes, we find that clustering algorithms function best with at least ~1% of the population sampled (or >50-100 cells) to anchor the unique markers that define its cluster. Basal/myoepithelial cells may be present in concentrations lower than 1% of total cells, below the threshold required to determine a unique cluster reliably within our current dataset. Another alternative could be that this small population of cells has a ‘quiescent’ transcriptome. Although we sequenced 100,000 reads/cell, this read depth may have been insufficient to detect low abundance transcripts. Milk protein genes in secretory MECs, such as LALBA and CSN2 , made up the bulk of the distribution of reads, possibly causing low abundance transcripts in the original population to go undetected. However, we do not anticipate that basal/myoepithelial cells express detectable levels of these milk protein transcripts. Therefore, it is more likely that, if these cell types are present in milk, they were below our detection threshold due to their low abundance in women with obesity, rather than limitations of our sequencing depth.
Previous reports showed expression of OCT4, SOX2 and NANOG by up to 96% of milk-derived cells [ 36 - 39 ], although formal evidence of stem cell function via teratoma formation remains lacking. We detected no cells with expression of these pluripotency markers. We did, however, observe partially differentiated luMECs which expressed both milk protein transcripts ( LALBA and CSN2 ) and markers of mammary progenitors ( CD44, CD55, ALDH1A3, ITGA6 and CD14 ). These may represent the cells previously described to differentiate into mammary cell types in vitro and deserve further investigation as potential experimental models of mammogenesis. Our pseudotime analysis also identified cells in a transitional state, early in pseudotime, which had substantial enrichment of ribosomal transcripts. These are reminiscent of MFG transcriptomes taken from women in the transitional stage of lactation. As these samples were taken from women at two weeks postpartum, and because we did not characterize their Na:K ratio, which most precisely identifies lactation stage [ 22 ], it is not possible to say whether these cells remained from the transition stage or if there is constant generation of mature secMECs, as continual removal of these cells into the milk might suggest. Sustained secMEC generation may require transitioning through a stage where the ribosomal machinery is produced, and we may have detected a population that is actively differentiating during mature lactation to replenish cells lost into the milk.
Lactation functional genes
At the peak of pseudotime amongst secMECs was cluster 5, which is characterized by prominent expression of almost all relevant transcripts of secreted milk products, with the major exception of LALBA , which was maximally expressed in cluster 0. These two clusters may represent functionally distinct secMECs and our findings introduce the possibility that cells in these clusters may have undergone semi-specialization with some MECs producing lactose and others producing milk proteins and lipids, as has previously been described in ruminants [ 69 ]. Evidence exists in the mouse for the asynchronous maturation of MECs within alveoli, suggesting that the MECs might engage in transient differentiation in their life cycle [ 70 ]. It remains unclear if these putative functionally distinct populations (i.e. clusters 0 and 5) have truly diverged as discrete secretory cell ‘subtypes,’ if differences in their transcriptomes reflect modulations in the output of the same type of cell, or if some form of transition from cells represented by cluster 0 into cells represented by cluster 5 exists [ 70 ]. Future studies are needed to determine whether there is a divergence in the maturation of these cells to the point where they are true cellular subsets, or if these cells transiently express various milk components at different points in time.
Interestingly, cluster 5 had very high expression of de novo fatty acid synthesis pathway genes, ACLY, ACACB, FASN, THRSP , and OLAH , genes that encode enzymes necessary for the medium chain fatty acids that are often the hallmark of milk fat composition [ 71 - 73 ]. Genes involved in triglyceride synthesis, including lipin 1 ( LPIN1 ), glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase, mitochondrial ( GPAM ), 1-acylglycerol-3-phosphate O-acyltransferase 1 ( AGPAT1 ), and diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 ( DGAT1 ) were expressed to a lesser degree than those genes involved in the de novo fatty acid synthesis pathway. One notable difference between these two functional categories is that enzymes of the de novo fatty acid synthesis pathway are soluble, cytoplasmic proteins, whereas the terminal enzymes for triglyceride synthesis are multi-span proteins resident to the endoplasmic reticulum. It is tempting to speculate that the mammary gland has adapted high expression levels of proteins that are lost during milk secretion, such as de novo fatty acid synthesis enzymes and enzymes of lactose synthesis as a result of inclusion in cytoplasmic crescents that form during apocrine secretion of milk lipids [ 25 ]. Conversely, transcripts of genes encoding endoplasmic reticulum membrane enzymes that catalyze triglyceride, cholesterol and phospholipid synthesis might be expressed at lower levels because their translated products are not lost during secretion.
Milk-derived MECs
Two mechanisms by which cells may be released from the epithelium include exfoliation and extrusion. Historically, cells of the mammary epithelium were thought to be exfoliated into the milk, however this process has yet to be fully described. Exfoliation is generally thought of as the loss of dead cells in the outermost layer of a stratified, or multi-layer, epithelium, whereas, the mammary alveolar epithelium exists as a single luminal layer, surrounded by a single basal myoepithelial layer. Therefore, it is difficult to imagine how exfoliation might occur without substantial damage to the integrity of the epithelium. Nevertheless, short term epithelial damage occurs in cows during milking [ 74 ], supporting the possibility that some cells found in human milk may result from damage to the epithelium during breastfeeding. Alternatively, MECs in milk could originate by extrusion of living cells from the mammary epithelium, a regulated process that occurs to avoid overcrowding and preserve the integrity of the epithelial barrier [ 75 ]. Whether milk-derived cells are exfoliated or extruded from the epithelium, there remains the question of their viability and functionality. We selected against dead cells by using a viability dye to remove cells with membranes that were permeable, and we did not detect any transcriptional signatures of apoptosis or anoikis by scRNA-seq in the remaining impermeable cells (data not shown). Still, without directly comparing the transcriptomes of milk-derived cells with dissociated cells isolated from biopsies of the lactating human mammary gland, we are unable to fully address origin mechanisms of MECs in milk. Furthermore, it is interesting that the population of cells in cluster 5 was about 4% of total cells, while cluster 0 was populated by >35% of all cells analyzed ( Sup. Tab. 5 ). This observation could suggest that the population of cells present in milk could represent ones that have been not selectively retained by the intact mammary gland. The significance of potential differences between milk-derived cells and the intact lactating mammary gland is the subject of future investigations.
The mechanisms by which MECs enter milk potentially affect the variability of cell types to be found there. With respect to the timeframe of a single feeding session, the temporal appearance of cells in milk mirrors that of milk lipids [ 76 ], and the abundance of cells in milk is increased following the oxytocin-induced milk ejection reflex in humans, mice and cows [ 76 - 78 ]. It is unknown if the cellular identities in milk change over the course of the feed. For example, luminal cells might be removed from the epithelium earlier in a feed than basal cells, which may be more resistant to becoming dislodged. We did not detect a basal population in the milk samples we collected, but as we did not standardize milk collections regarding the time of feed, we may not have detected these cells if such a temporal component regulates the appearance of basal cells in milk over the course of a feed. This possibility may explain the disparity seen between our results and previous accounts of pluripotent stem cells in milk [ 32 - 39 ], which are largely located in the basal layer. Another potential scenario might entail cells found in foremilk which were removed during or following the previous feed, and stored in the lumen, potentially becoming pre-apoptotic, while the cells in hindmilk might be more recently removed. Other contributors of variability may include intraindividual differences amongst cell types across lactation stages. There is extensive intraindividual variability in the cellular concentration of milk [ 76 ], and the concentration of immune cells declines in the transition from colostrum to mature milk, except in cases of maternal or infant infection [ 28 - 31 ]. As our study was designed to be exploratory, we were not powered to deeply phenotype the wide range of cellular diversity across individuals, lactation stage or time of feed. Rather, we intended to minimize variability by characterizing cells from two parity- and BMI-matched women, at the same stage in lactation (two weeks postpartum) and approximately the same time of day.
Future applications
The success of a woman’s lactation cycle has been shown to have substantial implications for both her and her child for the rest of their lives. Impaired lactation increases maternal risk for Type 2 diabetes (T2D), cardiovascular disease and breast cancer [ 79 ], with T2D risk especially elevated for those with prior GDM [ 19 , 80 ]. The preterm infant is most acutely at risk in the absence of breastfeeding, by way of susceptibility to the fatal gastrointestinal disease necrotizing enterocolitis [ 81 ]. Term infants are at elevated risk of obesity and insulin resistance beginning in childhood [ 82 , 11 , 83 ] if they are not breastfed. However, poor milk supply is a primary biological factor driving the decision to cease breastfeeding [ 84 ]. This complaint is especially prevalent in women who have obesity and/or insulin resistance during their reproductive years [ 85 , 86 , 7 ], precisely the mothers who cannot afford to not breastfeed.
Interventions aimed at improving lactation rates in women with obesity have had minimal success when providing intensive lactation counseling [ 87 , 88 ]. This is due largely to persisting gaps in knowledge of the basic biological mechanisms regulating human lactation initiation and milk production that limit development of effective interventions to improve breastfeeding outcomes, particularly among women with obesity and/or insulin resistance. One very informative approach toward assessing human lactation has been to characterize MFGs, because cytoplasmic RNA is included with MFGs during secretion from MECs. Microarrays and bulk RNA sequencing represent alternatives to scRNA-seq to interrogate transcriptional signatures in studies evaluating physiological changes in the mammary gland related to maternal phenotype [ 22 , 20 ], however, experiments utilizing MFG RNA are not capable of defining differences in the cellular heterogeneity or molecular diversity among the potential secMEC subtypes.
Previous attempts to compare milk-derived cells to mammary tissue have yielded less than ideal correlations [ 89 , 23 ]. This is likely due to heterogeneity of the milk-derived cells included in these comparisons. Surface marker-based cell sorting may increase the fidelity of milk-derived cells to intact luMECs. This may include removal of permeable (dead and/or dying), immune and endothelial cells. Unfortunately, our data suggest that sorting for EPCAM + /CD49f − cells may not yield pure populations of mature luMECs as indicated in the non-lactating mammary gland. Low EPCAM expression was also found in human milk-derived cells by Twigger, et al. [ 90 ] and in the dissociated mouse mammary gland during lactating as compared to other reproductive stages [ 40 ], consistent with the decline of EPCAM expression identified in MECs upon terminal differentiation in vitro [ 67 ]. These factors indicate that a new strategy for MEC isolation should be developed to isolate mature MECs and mammary progenitors from human milk. This approach may combine staining of the abovementioned progenitor markers and an alternative to EPCAM, such as LALBA and CSN2. The utility of these cells will be maximized when they are not permeabilized for staining of intracellular markers, however we were unable to identify any transcripts encoding cell surface markers that were highly expressed and specific for mature MECs (data not shown). The ready availability of milk-derived cells, and their characterization as secMECs and alveolar progenitor suggests that they may be useful in experimental approaches to interrogate the underlying molecular and cellular biology of lactation.
Conclusion
We propose that our scRNA-seq-based approach may assist mammary biologists in future studies investigating a wide variety of maternal phenotypes using current computational tools. Herein, we explored cryopreservation as a method which may greatly increase the feasibility of human lactation studies and we provide our computational framework to replicate these analyses with future single cell samples. In this way, our scRNA-seq results represent both a challenge and an opportunity. While our results do not point toward a clear path for isolation of distinct MEC populations without the need for downstream validation techniques such as intracellular staining, tissue culture or metabolomics, we anticipate that a wide variety of hypotheses could be tested using scRNA-seq, especially as sequencing costs continue to decrease. Possibilities include studies of maternal metabolic factors which may impact the volume and composition of milk produced, a topic we are particularly interested in. Application of scRNA-seq technology in the field of mammary gland biology has potential to provide greatly detailed information on the function of human lactation and as a potent technique to generate hypotheses for future research.
