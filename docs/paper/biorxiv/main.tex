\begin{abstract}
Single-cell RNA-seq produces cell type marker genes, yet they often fail to reproduce across studies. Part of this failure is how markers are reported: reported markers reflect author judgment as well as marker strength in the data, cell type and gene labels vary across papers, and flat cell type--gene pairs discard the comparison that produced them. We address each in turn. In \texttt{LLMarkers}, a benchmark we curated from seven studies, reported markers could not be reconstructed from the underlying differentially expressed genes alone, so they must be read from the papers that report them. A large language model (LLM) did this roughly fourfold more accurately by extracting markers from manuscript text than by producing them from cell type names or ranked gene tables. We therefore built \texttt{mrkr}, a tool that uses an LLM to extract reported markers from papers and maps their labels deterministically to stable identifiers, and applied it to 979 papers. Stable identifiers connected markers that label matching missed, yet markers reported for the same cell type still diverged. Changing only the comparison background reduced recovery of reported markers from 74\% to 21\%. A marker gene is therefore valid relative to the comparisons that produced it. Reusable markers require recording the comparison background with stable identifiers, which our \texttt{.onto} format provides.

\end{abstract}
\vspace{1em}
\end{@twocolumnfalse}

\section{Introduction}
Single-cell RNA sequencing identifies cell types by comparing gene expression across cells in an experiment \citep{Trapnell2015CellTypesStates,Wagner2016CellIdentity,Regev2017HumanCellAtlas,Heumos_2023}. A marker gene is therefore not an inherent property of a cell type. It is the result of a comparison in which a gene ranks as a marker only relative to gene expression measured in the other cells in the experiment \citep{Pullin2024MarkerSelection}. Adding groups of cells to a single-cell analysis can change which genes are markers. Yet markers are routinely treated as atomic and reusable, reported in papers and curated in databases as flat cell type--gene pairs, discarding the comparison that produced them \citep{Franzn_2019,Hu2022CellMarker}.

\input{tex-figures/fig1_mrkr_overview}

This reporting practice creates three problems. The first is that a reported marker is not the output of a computation. A differential-expression table ranks thousands of genes, and the authors report a handful of them, chosen with prior knowledge of the cell type and of markers used in earlier work. Whether reported markers can be recovered from the data alone is therefore an open question, and the answer decides whether markers can be regenerated from data or must be extracted from the papers that report them.

The second is that labels are unstable \citep{Miller2020CellNomenclature,OsumiSutherland2021CellOntologies}. The same population of cells may be called ``plasma B cell'' in one paper and ``plasma cell'' in another, and ``red blood cell'' in one paper and ``erythrocyte'' in another. Genes also appear under different names, such as ``DNGR-1'' and \textit{CLEC9A}. Stable identifiers can resolve these differences---the Cell Ontology for cell types and Ensembl for genes \citep{OsumiSutherland2021CellOntologies,Cunningham2022Ensembl}---but papers rarely report them \citep{Lubiana2022MIRACL}.

The third is that stable identifiers do not state the comparison background, the cells against which a cell type was compared. Knowing that two studies both report \textit{CD8A} (\texttt{ENSG00000153563}) as a marker for CD8-positive, alpha-beta T cells (\texttt{CL:0000625}) does not tell us which cells each study compared. Since markers are defined by comparison, a marker stripped of its comparison no longer states what it was shown to distinguish. A marker derived from sorted peripheral blood mononuclear cells and one derived from a whole-tissue atlas may share a name but arise from different comparisons.

These three reporting problems are distinct from, but related to, biological and technical variability in scRNA-seq data. Assay chemistry, sequencing depth, quantification, normalization, replicate structure, donor variation, disease state, and differential-expression methods can change which genes best distinguish a cell type from the others \citep{Heumos_2023,Soneson_2018,Squair_2021,Krzak2026CrohnReplicability}. The flat cell type--gene reporting scheme records neither these experimental details nor the comparison background.

Effective marker reuse therefore requires reading markers from the papers that report them, stable identifiers to establish that studies refer to the same cell type and gene, and the comparison background to establish what each reported marker gene was shown to distinguish.

In this work, we ask how well reported marker genes can be recovered, connected, and reused across studies, and what information must be retained to support reuse. We proceed in three steps. First, using a manually curated benchmark, we compare reported markers with their differential-expression strength in the underlying data and test whether a large language model (LLM) can produce the same cell type--gene associations from cell type names, from ranked DE tables, or from the manuscript text. Second, we introduce \texttt{mrkr}, a tool that extracts reported cell type--marker gene associations from papers, normalizes each reported label, and maps it deterministically to stable identifiers from the Cell Ontology and Ensembl (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:mrkr_overview}}). Each association is stored in the \texttt{.onto} format, which records the reported and normalized labels, stable identifiers, supporting sentence, and any comparison background named in the paper. Third, we apply \texttt{mrkr} to 979 papers. Stable identifiers connect reported associations that exact label matching misses, but the sets of reported markers still diverge across studies. A controlled analysis that holds the target cells fixed while changing the comparison background shows why: identifiers establish which genes and cell types can be connected, while the comparison background establishes what each marker was shown to distinguish. Effective marker reuse requires both.


\section{Results}
\subsection*{Authors exercise considerable judgment in selecting markers to report}

If scRNA-seq marker genes are fully derived from data, then reported markers should be recoverable from the differential expression computed in each study. Testing this requires a record of which markers a paper reported. \claim{benchmark-summary}{We manually curated 2{,}528 reported cell type--gene associations from seven scRNA-seq studies spanning six tissues, together with each study's differential-expression (DE) tables, and call this benchmark \texttt{LLMarkers}. The benchmark covers 168 cell types, 641 genes, and 1{,}560 unique cell type--gene associations (\textbf{Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}}).}\source{analysis/artifacts/benchmark_evidence_v1/reconciliation.json}\source{analysis/benchmark_verification.ipynb} \claim{benchmark-modality}{Most markers were reported only in figures: 1{,}151 of the 1{,}560 associations (74\%), with 256 (16\%) only in text and 153 (10\%) in both (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:marker_selection}}A).}\source{analysis/benchmark_verification.ipynb}

\input{tex-figures/fig2_marker_recovery}

Reported markers could not be fully reconstructed from the DE tables. \claim{benchmark-deg-overlap}{The fraction of reported markers present in a study's DE tables ranged from 18\% in the embryonic skeletal development study \citep{He2021} to 97\% in the idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis study \citep{Adams2020} (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:marker_selection}}A). In the Emont adipose study, the authors report \textit{PDGFRA} as a marker of subpopulations \texttt{hASPC1} through \texttt{hASPC6}, but the DE table for those subpopulations includes it only for \texttt{hASPC1}.}\source{analysis/benchmark_verification.ipynb} The same marker could also appear in two tables with fold changes in opposite directions. \claim{benchmark-sign-contradictions}{Among 544 marker gene associations found in at least two DE tables from the same study, 46 (8.5\%) were upregulated in one table and downregulated in the other.}\source{analysis/benchmark_verification.ipynb} The direction of a reported marker depends on which cells it was compared to, and each table is a different comparison.

Markers present in the DE tables were not simply the top-ranked genes. \claim{expert-ranks}{Among markers reported in text, 53\% appeared in the top 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs); among markers reported in figures, 50\%. The top 100 DEGs contained 66\% and 62\%.}\source{analysis/artifacts/fig2_rank_recovery_summary.tsv}\source{analysis/build_fig2_marker_recovery.py}\source{analysis/lfc_comparison.ipynb} No single rank cutoff separated reported markers from the rest. \claim{expert-optimal-n}{Averaged across studies, F-score peaked at only 0.41 for markers reported in text and 0.42 for markers reported in figures, near cutoffs of 30 and 40 genes (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:marker_selection}B}), and per-study optimal cutoffs ranged from 21 to 186 genes.}\source{analysis/artifacts/fig2_rank_recovery_summary.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/fig2_optimal_deg_cutoff.tsv}\source{analysis/build_fig2_marker_recovery.py} Within the Hildreth study alone, rank and precision--recall distributions varied by cell type (\textbf{Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:hildreth_detail}}). Differential expression is often described as a marker selection process, but our results indicate that authors do not simply select the top-ranked genes, so marker selection cannot be reconstructed from ranking the underlying data alone.

\subsection*{LLMs extract markers better than they generate or select them}

Marker genes could not be fully reconstructed from DEG rankings, so we asked whether an LLM could produce them. We compared three LLM tasks. The first prompted the model to generate markers from cell type names alone. The second prompted the model to select markers from ranked DE tables. The third prompted the model to extract reported markers from manuscript text (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:llm_curation}}; \textbf{Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:llm_curation}}). The tasks differ in how much of each paper the model sees (none for the first task, the DE tables for the second, and the full text for the third).

Extraction recovered reported markers more accurately than generation or selection. \claim{gen-f1}{Generation from cell type names reached a mean cell type--gene F1 of 0.17 against the markers reported in text.}\source{analysis/artifacts/fig2_llm_recovery.tsv}\source{analysis/build_fig2_marker_recovery.py} \claim{sel-f1}{Selection from ranked DE tables reached 0.16 at the best input depth for each study.}\source{analysis/artifacts/fig2_llm_recovery.tsv}\source{analysis/build_fig2_marker_recovery.py} \claim{ext-text-f1}{Extraction reached a mean F1 of 0.64, and 0.61 when the model also identified the DE table supporting each association.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_summary.json}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_papers.tsv} Extraction is scored against the same text it reads, so part of its advantage is by construction. That construction is the goal of curation: to preserve what the authors reported rather than predict it. As a consequence, accurate marker gene recovery required reading the paper, which supports using LLMs to extract and normalize reported markers.

We implemented LLM-based marker extraction in \texttt{mrkr}, a command-line tool that converts manuscript text into a validated \texttt{.onto} file (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:mrkr_overview}}). \claim{mrkr-record-contract}{Each retained association links the supporting sentence to one organism, one cell type label, at least one marker gene, the marker direction, and any tissue or comparison background named in the paper. Validation checks that the supporting sentence and every extracted term occur exactly in the manuscript (Methods). The LLM extracts named cell types and genes and produces normalized labels that are mapped, deterministically, to stable identifiers from Ensembl, NCBI Taxonomy, Cell Ontology, and UBERON.}\source{analysis/mine_corpus.py}\source{analysis/build_claim_db.py}\source{README.md} This separation isolates the variability of author naming from the assignment of stable identifiers, so label mappings can be audited and revised without changing what the paper reported.


\subsection*{Stable identifiers connect markers across papers}

To test whether stable identifiers connect markers across papers, we applied \texttt{mrkr} to 504 bioRxiv preprints and 475 papers linked to the Human Cell Atlas. \claim{corpus-source-grounded-summary}{Of the 979 papers, 721 reported at least one cell type--gene association, totaling 11{,}336 associations of 27{,}713 marker genes. 586 of the 11{,}336 associations (5.2\%) named a comparison background.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/corpus_summary.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/term_coverage.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/report.md}

\claim{corpus-gene-mapping}{Of the 27{,}713 reported marker genes, 26{,}919 (97\%) mapped to an Ensembl identifier.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/corpus_summary.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/term_coverage.tsv} Cell type labels mapped less often. We accepted a Cell Ontology identifier only when a normalized label matched a canonical label or an official exact synonym. \claim{corpus-identifier-linkage}{Of 3{,}355 distinct cell type labels, 294 matched a canonical label, 112 matched an exact synonym, and the remaining 2{,}949 (88\%) matched neither. The 406 accepted labels (12\%) covered 363 identifiers.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/cell_ontology_label_audit.tsv} Gene names largely followed a shared nomenclature while cell type names did not. But despite the low mapping rate, stable identifiers showed how variable naming conventions collapse onto single cell types (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:corpus_marker_reuse}}A). \claim{corpus-label-collapse}{``T cell,'' ``T-cell,'' ``T lymphocyte,'' and ``T-lymphocyte'' all mapped to \texttt{CL:0000084}. Overall, 324 of the 363 identifiers received one reported label, 37 received two, and two identifiers received four.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/cell_ontology_label_audit.tsv}

To compare markers across papers, we first pooled the genes reported for each normalized cell type label in each paper. We call this set the paper's marker gene panel. Two panels from different articles that used the same normalized label form a panel pair. \claim{corpus-analysis-ready}{Keeping panels with at least three genes produced 3{,}454 panels and 10{,}437 panel pairs.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/label_profiles_analysis.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/same_label_pairs.tsv}

Stable identifiers connected panels that exact label matching missed (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:corpus_marker_reuse}}B). \claim{corpus-label-signal}{Twelve identifiers connected 248 panel pairs with different normalized labels, such as ``plasma B cell'' and ``plasma cell,'' and 113 of the 248 (45.6\%) shared at least one marker. Panel pairs whose labels matched exactly shared a marker at a similar rate (49.2\% with a shared identifier, 54.1\% without, after matching by paper source and panel size). Panel pairs with different identifiers shared a marker 1.6\% of the time.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/same_label_pairs.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/identifier_recovered_pairs.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/identifier_recovered_matched_summary.tsv} Marker sharing never exceeded 55\% in any group because papers report different cells in different tissues and disease states, under different cell type comparisons. Therefore, stable identifiers recover associations that exact labels miss but a shared identifier does not establish that the sampled cell groups are biologically identical or that the cell type and markers associated with it are reusable.

\subsection*{Marker panels vary with comparison background}

Papers rarely state the comparison background directly, but the cell types a paper reports indicate which cells it observed, and so approximate the background against which each marker was computed. If reported markers depend on the comparison background, then papers that observed similar cell types should report more similar markers (\textbf{Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:paper_celltype_joint}}). Cell type labels reported in a paper provide a limited proxy for the cell types each paper observed (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:corpus_marker_reuse}}C). \claim{corpus-coreported-context}{Across the 10{,}437 panel pairs, marker sharing was weakly positively associated with the similarity of the co-reported labels (Pearson $r=0.149$).}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/same_label_pairs.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/coreported_context_metrics.tsv} Papers that reported more similar sets of cell types tended to report more similar marker panels. The association is weak in part because co-reported labels are an imperfect proxy in that they do not fully identify the comparison background that generated the marker.

To test whether persistent markers emerge as curated marker sets accumulate, we took the 37 normalized labels reported in at least ten papers, added panels one paper at a time in random order, and asked whether any marker remained in every panel. Each additional paper contributes markers from a different comparison background. If a cell type label named one fixed marker panel, combining papers would preserve a shared core of markers. Shared markers were lost as papers accumulated (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:corpus_marker_reuse}}D). \claim{corpus-marker-accumulation}{After two papers, an estimated 51.8\% of labels retained at least one shared marker. The fraction fell to 28.8\% after three papers, 11.3\% after five, and 1.7\% after ten. Repeating the analysis with 27 accepted Cell Ontology identifiers gave nearly identical results.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/label_intersection_accumulation.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/ontology_intersection_accumulation.tsv} A cell type label or identifier does not specify a marker panel that remains stable as papers accumulate.

\subsection*{Changing the comparison background changes marker recovery}

The marker accumulation results point to the necessity of the comparison background for defining a cell type marker. But isolating the effect of the comparison background requires holding the cell types and the measurements fixed while changing only the background (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:cap_partition}}A). This is generally challenging but the Cell Annotation Platform (CAP) of the Human Cell Atlas provides a close enough proxy to this design. They host data for one study that contains a liver myeloid analysis nested within an all-cell liver analysis of the same cells. The myeloid analysis separates ten myeloid cell types, while the all-cell analysis, on the same cells, adds non-myeloid cells and groups the myeloid cells under coarser labels, resulting in 17 total cell types. Comparing the two datasets therefore changes the groups entering each differential-expression calculation while the measurement stays fixed.

In the CAP myeloid cell types, reported markers were recovered more often in the analysis they were curated from (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:cap_partition}}B). \claim{cap-liver-local-global-de}{Across the ten myeloid labels, one-vs-rest differential expression within the myeloid analysis recovered a mean of 74\% of reported markers in the top 100 genes. The all-cell analysis recovered 21\%. Recovery was higher in the myeloid analysis for every label.}\source{analysis/artifacts/cap_liver_local_global_deg_summary.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/cap_liver_local_global_recovery_subsampling_summary.tsv}\source{analysis/build_cap_liver_local_global_deg.py} \claim{cap-liver-rank-overlap}{The two analyses also produced different rankings: for every label, the top 100 DEGs of the two analyses overlapped by less than half (Jaccard 0.05--0.48; \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:cap_partition}}B).}\source{analysis/artifacts/cap_liver_local_global_deg_summary.tsv} \claim{cap-liver-barcode-constant}{Defining each all-cell target group by the exact barcodes of its myeloid cells, rather than by the study's all-cell labels, gave the same result: a mean recovery of 22\% (\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:cap_partition}}C).}\source{analysis/artifacts/cap_liver_barcode_constant_recovery.tsv}\source{analysis/build_cap_liver_barcode_constant_deg.py} The drop is therefore driven by the change in comparison background, not by differences in how the cells were labeled. Changing the comparison background changed which reported markers were recovered, while the cell types and measurements stayed fixed.

\subsection*{Marker sets are defined by their comparison backgrounds}

The dependence of markers on comparison backgrounds can be stated exactly. For a study $s$, partition $p$, target cell group $t$, and comparison background $h$, let $M_{s,p}(t,h)$ be the genes called as markers for $t$ against $h$. A single marker gene for a set of comparison backgrounds $C$ must pass every comparison,
\begin{align}
  M_{s,p}(t;C) = \bigcap_{h\in C} M_{s,p}(t,h),
\end{align}
while a marker gene panel $P$ needs only to cover every comparison: $P\cap M_{s,p}(t,h)\neq\varnothing$ for each $h\in C$. A cell type is then a group of cells identified by those markers (subject to the stated comparisons).

We formalized these definitions and their consequences in the Supplementary Note. \claim{formal-marker-definition}{The formalization proves that marker gene sets are intersections of pairwise marker sets, marker gene panels are hitting sets over the comparisons, and adding a comparison can only remove single-gene markers or add requirements to a panel.}\source{analysis/formal/MarkerIdentifiability/Basic.lean}\source{analysis/formal/CLAIMS.md}

\claim{formal-pooled-vs-pairwise}{We further prove that one-vs-all DE testing is not mathematically equivalent to testing against each group pairwise. A pooled one-vs-rest statistic averages the non-target groups, so a gene can pass the pooled test while failing the comparison against one specific group.}\source{analysis/formal/MarkerIdentifiability/Basic.lean}\source{analysis/formal/CLAIMS.md} The formal result also explains hierarchical markers. A marker that passes a broad set of comparisons remains valid when the set is restricted, but a marker found among closely related cell groups need not identify the broader parent group, as illustrated in the CAP analysis \citep{Domcke2023ReferenceCellTree,Beltz2026ImmuneHierarchy}.

The formalization also clarifies the role of stable identifiers for defining marker genes. Stable identifiers make $t$ and $h$ joinable across papers, but a shared label or identifier does not imply a shared marker panel (\textbf{Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:paper_celltype_joint}}). The utility of \texttt{mrkr} is to normalize labels and assign the stable identifiers that make this join possible.

\section{Discussion}

Marker genes are a standard endpoint of a single-cell RNA-seq experiment. They are the discriminating features that attach cell type labels to clusters, and they are expected to transfer between studies. Often they do not, because cell type and gene labels vary across papers, because reported markers mix data with the authors' prior knowledge and judgment and cannot be fully reconstructed from the data alone, and because markers depend on their comparison background, which the flat cell type--gene format discards.

Large language models can address the first two problems. In our benchmark, extraction was roughly fourfold more accurate than generating markers from cell type names or selecting them from ranked DE tables. \texttt{mrkr} uses an LLM to curate normalized marker labels into an \texttt{.onto} file, and each extracted marker keeps the supporting sentence that reported the association. Deterministic mapping to stable identifiers then joins associations reported under different names across papers, at a scale no manual curation can match. This solves the naming problem, the first step toward reuse. \claim{corpus-resource-release}{We release the resulting corpus of 979 validated \texttt{.onto} files, their validation metadata, and a derived SQLite database.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_onto_v2.tar.gz.sha256}\source{README.md}

The third problem, tracking the comparison background, is harder. Even with stable identifiers, markers are context dependent. Shared markers for the same cell type vanished as evidence accumulated, and changing only the comparison background changed which reported markers were recovered. An LLM can extract the comparison background, but only when a paper states it, and most papers do not. Our curation cannot fix that. In that sense, the missing comparison backgrounds are a sociological problem that manifests as a reproducibility issue. The fix is to deploy \texttt{mrkr} at authoring time, while the comparison background is still known, recording the reported label, normalized label, stable identifier, supporting sentence, and comparison background together in an \texttt{.onto} file.

\balance

Knowing the comparison background and reusing stable identifiers are steps toward resolving the debate over the definition of a cell type \citep{Trapnell2015CellTypesStates,OsumiSutherland2021CellOntologies}. All sides of that debate agree that a cell type should be defined by what is measured, but the debate has lacked a pragmatic resolution. We propose that marker genes, recorded with their comparison background, are that resolution. We prove that a marker gene is valid relative to the comparisons that produced it and that a marker panel covers those comparisons. This definition can be shared because it names exactly what must be recorded for a marker to be reused. While these reporting details cannot remove assay variation or supply cell groups that were never observed, they make the comparison behind each marker observable and auditable. They prevent missing comparison information from being confused with those limitations. This is what \texttt{mrkr} and \texttt{.onto} fix.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Benchmark construction and verification}

\claim{methods-benchmark-schema}{The LLMarkers benchmark was assembled by manual curation of seven single-cell RNA-seq studies spanning six tissues: \texttt{adipose} \citep{Emont2022,Hildreth2021}, \texttt{bone} \citep{He2021}, \texttt{eye} \citep{Gautam2021}, \texttt{lung} \citep{Adams2020}, \texttt{ovary} \citep{Wagner2020}, and \texttt{testis} \citep{Shamis2020}. The benchmark was designed as a carefully linked evaluation set rather than a comprehensive survey of single-cell reporting styles. For each study, a curator read the manuscript and recorded each marker gene--cell type association, its verbatim sentence or figure evidence, source type, linked differential-expression table when available, organism, and Ensembl gene identifier. We preserved the original flat records and converted them losslessly into separate text statements, image statements, and differential-expression tables. Repeated source rows remain traceable through record identifiers rather than being discarded. Ten mouse records from the Adams study remain in the all-organism benchmark totals; the current extraction evaluation is restricted to human records.}\source{analysis/build_benchmark_evidence.py}\source{analysis/artifacts/benchmark_evidence_v1/reconciliation.json}

\claim{methods-deg-parsing}{Each study's supplementary tables were parsed to extract differential expression gene lists. Header detection was performed automatically by scanning the first 10 rows for a row containing expected column names (e.g., gene, p\_value, avg\_log2FC). The resulting DEG lists were stored in the same evidence schema as human annotations, enabling direct comparison.}\source{analysis/benchmark_verification.ipynb}

We verified the converted benchmark against both the source records and manuscript text. \claim{methods-verification}{Every source row round-tripped exactly and every source-file hash was unchanged. Of 166 primary text evidence claims, 158 (95\%) were anchored to exact manuscript offsets; the remaining eight are retained with an explicit unanchored status rather than treated as verified text.}\source{analysis/artifacts/benchmark_evidence_v1/reconciliation.json}\source{analysis/build_benchmark_evidence.py}

\subsection{Marker strength analysis}

\claim{methods-marker-strength-matching}{To quantify where human-curated marker genes fall in the differential expression ranking, we matched each marker to its corresponding entry in the dataset's DEG list. For human-curated markers, matching was performed on the four-part key (dataset, data\_id, group\_name, feature\_id), requiring exact agreement on the cell type, gene, and supplementary data source. Markers that could not be matched---because the gene was absent from the DEG list or lacked an Ensembl ID---were excluded from this analysis.}\source{analysis/gen_strength.ipynb}

\claim{methods-marker-rank-summary}{For each matched marker, we recorded its rank within the cell type's DEG list (rank 1 = highest log-fold change) and its log-fold change. We computed the fraction of markers falling within the top $N$ DEGs at every threshold $N$ from 1 to 200, reported the values at $N=50$ and $N=100$, and separately tabulated recovery for text-sourced and image-sourced markers.}\source{analysis/lfc_comparison.ipynb}


\claim{methods-optimal-deg-depth}{To estimate the optimal DEG list depth for recovering each marker set, we computed precision--recall curves by varying a rank threshold $N$ from 1 to 20{,}000. At each threshold, we defined the top $N$ DEGs as predicted markers and computed precision, recall, and the F-score. The optimal $N$ was defined as the threshold that maximized the F-score. This analysis was performed separately for each dataset and marker source (text or image).}\source{analysis/artifacts/fig2_optimal_deg_cutoff.tsv}\source{analysis/build_fig2_marker_recovery.py}



\subsection{LLM generation and extraction}

\claim{methods-llm-run-settings}{We evaluated three approaches to LLM-based marker gene curation across the seven benchmark datasets. All experiments used Claude (claude-opus-4-6) with temperature 0 and a maximum output length of 32{,}000 tokens; we did not benchmark model-to-model variation. Costs were computed from the model's API pricing (\$5 per million input tokens, \$25 per million output tokens for claude-opus-4-6).}\source{analysis/build_fig2_marker_recovery.py}\source{analysis/selection_analysis.ipynb}

\claim{methods-llm-generation}{In the generation experiment (celltypes-to-genes), we gave the model the list of cell type names from each dataset's human-curated marker set and asked it to generate 5--15 well-established marker genes per cell type. The model received only the species and cell type names---no manuscript text, figures, or supplementary data. In a second generation experiment (genes-to-celltypes), we extracted the gene list for each cell type from the human-curated set, replaced the cell type names with anonymous labels (Group~1, Group~2, \ldots), and asked the model to predict the cell type for each group. We measured prediction accuracy as the fraction of exact string matches between predicted and original cell type names. Non-exact predictions were counted as related when they showed substring containment or shared label tokens; remaining predictions were counted as having no lexical relation.}\source{analysis/ext_vs_gen.ipynb}

\claim{methods-llm-selection}{In the selection experiment, we parsed each dataset's DEG tables, ranked genes per cell type by absolute log fold change, and presented the top $N$ genes (with log fold change and adjusted $p$-value) to the model, asking it to select which are true markers. We swept $N \in \{10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500\}$ for each dataset and reported the $N$ that maximized pair-level F1. As a control for whether cell type identity aids selection, we repeated the full sweep with cell type names replaced by anonymous cluster labels (Cluster~1, Cluster~2, \ldots), preserving the gene lists and statistics but removing cell type identity from the names.}\source{analysis/selection_analysis.ipynb}\source{analysis/build_fig2_marker_recovery.py}

To quantify the best possible selection performance, we computed a DEG-constrained max F1 score at each $N$. Let $H$ be the set of human-curated marker pairs and $D_N$ be the set of marker pairs available in the top-$N$ DEG lists shown to the model. The recoverable set is $R_N = H \cap D_N$. Any selector restricted to $D_N$ cannot recover pairs outside $R_N$. The maximum achievable pair-level F1 is therefore
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{F1}_{\max}(N) = \frac{2|R_N|}{|H| + |R_N|},
\end{align}
which is attained by selecting exactly the recoverable set $R_N$ (precision $=1$). In \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:llm_curation}}C, the selection upper bound is plotted as open circles at each dataset's selected operating point, using that same dataset-specific $N$; the dashed line marks their mean.

\claim{methods-llm-extraction}{In the joint extraction experiment, Claude received the full manuscript and a catalog mapping each candidate \texttt{data\_id} to the cell type labels present in that paper's DEG sources. For every reported human marker association, the model returned the cell type, gene, verbatim supporting text, and one candidate \texttt{data\_id}, or null when no source was supported. The model did not receive the human annotations. When it returned null, an exact target-label match supplied the narrowest candidate source; this affected five of 152 retained claims. We converted the output to the current \texttt{mrkr} claim structure and coalesced records with the same source text and target into marker panels.}\source{analysis/evaluate_mrkr_joint_deg_extraction.py}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_papers.tsv}

\claim{methods-llm-evaluation-subset}{The extraction evaluation used positive human text annotations. Validation required every retained claim to have an exact manuscript offset and every retained marker gene to occur explicitly in that source text. Unsupported genes and claims with ambiguous source offsets were excluded. Gene labels were mapped programmatically with the packaged \texttt{gmap.txt}. We computed macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 across studies for exact normalized cell type--gene pairs and exact \texttt{data\_id}--cell type--gene triples. The same extracted terms were used for both scores.}\source{analysis/evaluate_mrkr_joint_deg_extraction.py}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_summary.json}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_pair_review.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_triple_review.tsv} \claim{ext-results}{Validation retained 152 associations listing 311 mapped marker genes and excluded five as unsupported or ambiguous.}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_papers.tsv}\source{analysis/evaluate_mrkr_joint_deg_extraction.py}

\claim{methods-llm-source-verification}{The joint experiment saved each raw model response before validation. Every derived artifact records hashes for the prompt, manuscript, human benchmark, DEG catalog, and gene map. The run is therefore resumable and fails if an input changes.}\source{analysis/evaluate_mrkr_joint_deg_extraction.py}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_benchmark_pilot_20260721/joint_deg_extraction_v1/joint_extraction_report.md}

\subsection{Paper corpora}

\claim{methods-biorxiv-mirror}{We selected bioRxiv preprints from a local mirror of 428{,}741 MECA archives downloaded from the bioRxiv source repository in January 2025. Each archive contains JATS XML and structured metadata.}\source{analysis/biorxiv_summary.ipynb}\source{analysis/build_marker_corpus.py} We required a CC-BY or CC0 license, terms that indicated human relevance, and terms related to cell types, single-cell RNA sequencing, or marker genes. We kept the most recent version for each DOI. \claim{methods-biorxiv-filter}{These filters produced 504 bioRxiv manuscripts.}\source{analysis/biorxiv_summary.ipynb}\source{analysis/build_marker_corpus.py} JATS XML was converted to Markdown while preserving section structure.

We assembled the second corpus from publications linked to Human Cell Atlas projects. Project metadata supplied publication titles, URLs, and DOIs. We deduplicated records by DOI and then by normalized title. \claim{methods-hca-publication-corpus}{Of 515 linked publication records, 475 had manuscript text that could be normalized to Markdown and processed.}\source{analysis/compile_hca_human_publications.py}\source{analysis/download_hca_manuscripts.py}\source{analysis/unify_hca_manuscripts.py}\source{data/hca/manuscripts_manifest.tsv}

\subsection{Label normalization and identifier grounding}

The corpus source manifest lists one paper identifier, paper source (bioRxiv or HCA), organism, manuscript path, and source hash per paper. We processed all 979 manuscripts with \texttt{mrkr} version 0.4.0 at the pinned source revision in \texttt{analysis/mrkr.lock}. Extraction used Claude (claude-opus-4-6). The model received the complete Markdown manuscript and the organism but could not assign database identifiers.

\claim{methods-corpus-mrkr-workflow}{For each paper, \texttt{mrkr extract} returned exact source text, normalized labels, and term offsets. \texttt{mrkr ground} mapped human genes with the packaged gene map, organisms to NCBI Taxonomy, cell types and comparisons to Cell Ontology, and tissues to UBERON. \texttt{mrkr validate} then checked the source hash, source offsets, explicit term offsets, required term counts, gene direction, and grounding metadata.}\source{analysis/mine_corpus.py}\source{analysis/mrkr.lock}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_onto_v2.tar.gz.sha256} Canonical Ensembl GRCh38 release 114 symbols take priority when a gene alias is ambiguous. Each saved \texttt{.onto} file records the model and response identifier, prompt digest, gene-map digest, ontology queries, selected identifiers, and validation state. The archive stores the raw model responses separately.

Some initial outputs contained individual associations or gene terms that failed exact-source validation. \claim{methods-corpus-repair}{We repaired these files without another model call. The repair removed only the failing association or conflicting gene term, recorded every removal, and reran grounding and validation.}\source{analysis/repair_corpus.py}\source{README.md} Papers with no retained association remained in the corpus as valid empty results. We built the analysis database from the final ontology manifest. The database stores paper metadata, reported cell type--gene associations, exact source text and offsets, normalized terms, identifiers, directions, and grounding state. Ontology hierarchy expansion was disabled for the primary analysis.

\subsection{Cross-paper links by labels and identifiers}

We analyzed mapped positive marker genes separately from mapped negative marker genes. A reported marker gene panel is the set of mapped positive genes assigned to one normalized cell type label in one paper. Labels were normalized with Unicode NFKC, case folding, replacement of non-word characters with spaces, and whitespace normalization. The primary analysis used exact normalized-label equality. It did not use substring, token-overlap, or semantic label matching. We retained panels with at least three mapped genes.

We grouped source records by normalized article title and excluded comparisons within the same article. For every cell type label reported in at least two articles, we compared all retained paper pairs. Marker similarity was the Jaccard similarity between the two sets of Ensembl gene identifiers. We also recorded whether the sets shared any gene and the fraction of each set recovered by the other.

\claim{methods-matched-label-controls}{For each pair of papers that used the same cell type label, we sampled one pair with different labels. Control panels matched the original paper source and the base-2 marker-count bin. We required different article keys and repeated the control sampling with 25 fixed random seeds.}\source{analysis/analyze_mrkr_corpus.py}\source{analysis/artifacts/mrkr_corpus_analysis_v1/matched_background_seed_sensitivity.tsv} Pair-weighted estimates give each paper pair equal weight. Label-balanced estimates first average the paper pairs for each label and then give each recurring label equal weight. We used 5{,}000 label-bootstrap replicates for the 95\% intervals among papers that used the same label. Control intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles across the 25 matched draws.

To measure cumulative marker-panel intersection, we fixed the cohort to normalized labels reported in at least ten papers. In each of 1{,}000 bootstrap replicates, we resampled labels with replacement, randomly ordered the paper-level panels for each selected label, and intersected the first one through ten panels. At each step, we recorded whether the intersection contained at least one marker. The plotted estimate is the mean fraction of labels with a nonempty intersection. \subsection{Cell Annotation Platform expression analysis}

\claim{methods-cap-liver-expression-analysis}{We used CAP dataset 1440 as the liver myeloid analysis and CAP dataset 1437 as the broader all-cell liver analysis. The files are nested, not independent. All 40{,}821 cell barcodes in dataset 1440 occur in the 167{,}598-cell all-cell file, which contains 17 author cell type labels.}\source{analysis/artifacts/cap_liver_nesting_check.tsv}\source{analysis/verify_cap_nesting.py} We loaded each file with \texttt{anndata.read\_h5ad}, used the \texttt{user\_provided} expression layer when present, and used \texttt{author\_cell\_type} as the label. We mapped each myeloid label to its broader author label in the all-cell analysis. \claim{methods-cap-label-agreement}{The two annotations disagree for some cells: 83.8\% of the myeloid cells carry their mapped broad label in the all-cell analysis. As a control, we recomputed the all-cell one-vs-rest rankings with each target group defined by the exact barcodes of the corresponding myeloid label instead of the study's all-cell labels; mean top-100 recovery was 21.7\%, matching the label-based 21\%.}\source{analysis/artifacts/cap_liver_nesting_check.tsv}\source{analysis/artifacts/cap_liver_barcode_constant_recovery.tsv}\source{analysis/build_cap_liver_barcode_constant_deg.py}

For each label, we ranked all genes by the difference in mean expression between the cell group of interest and the remaining cells, divided by its standard error. Reported markers came from the CAP marker JSON and were restricted to genes mapped to human Ensembl identifiers. Top-100 recovery is the fraction of reported markers among the first 100 ranked genes. Differential-expression stability is the Jaccard similarity between the myeloid and all-cell top-100 sets. Marker retrieval F1 treats the reported markers as positives and reports the maximum F1 over rank cutoffs.

For uncertainty in top-100 recovery, we performed 50 cell-type-stratified subsampling replicates. Each replicate sampled 80\% of cells within each author label, with at most 10{,}000 cells per label. Figure~\ref{fig:cap_partition}A shows the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
